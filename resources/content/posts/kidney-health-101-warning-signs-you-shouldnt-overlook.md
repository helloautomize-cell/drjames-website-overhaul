Kidney disease does not always announce itself with obvious symptoms. In many people, kidney function can decline for some time before they notice that anything is wrong. This is one reason warning signs such as persistent fatigue, swelling, changes in urination, or repeatedly foamy urine should not simply be dismissed.

The kidneys do much more than produce urine. They help remove waste from the blood, regulate fluid levels, maintain electrolyte balance, and contribute to blood pressure control. When kidney function is affected, several parts of the body can eventually be affected as well.

The good news is that [kidney problems](/conditions/kidney-problems/) can often be identified through relatively simple blood and urine tests. Recognising the warning signs and checking kidney function when appropriate can help doctors identify problems earlier and determine what needs to be done next.

- Persistent or unexplained tiredness

- Swelling around the ankles, feet, legs, or eyes

- Urine that remains unusually foamy

- Blood in the urine

- Changes in how often you urinate

- Waking frequently at night to urinate

- Nausea or reduced appetite

- Itchy or unusually dry skin

- Muscle cramps

- Changes in blood pressure

### What Do the Kidneys Actually Do?

Most people think of the kidneys simply as organs that make urine. Their role is much broader.

The kidneys continuously filter blood and help remove waste products and excess fluid through urine. They also help maintain the body's balance of minerals and electrolytes, contribute to blood pressure regulation, and support other important functions.

When kidney function falls, waste products and excess fluid may begin to accumulate. Depending on the underlying problem and how advanced it is, this can eventually lead to symptoms affecting several systems in the body.

That is why kidney health is closely connected with conditions such as diabetes and high blood pressure.

## What Are the Early Warning Signs of Kidney Disease?

Kidney disease can look different from one person to another. Some symptoms are relatively subtle, while others become more noticeable as kidney function declines.

Here are some changes worth discussing with a doctor.

### 1. Foamy or Frothy Urine

Occasional bubbles in the toilet are usually not a reason to panic. Urine can look bubbly because of the speed of the urine stream or other temporary factors.

Persistent or unusually frothy urine, however, deserves attention.

One possible reason is the presence of excess protein in the urine. Healthy kidneys normally prevent significant amounts of protein from passing into urine. When the filtering units of the kidneys are damaged, protein can leak through.

Foamy urine does not automatically mean that you have kidney disease. But if the change keeps happening, particularly when it occurs alongside swelling or other symptoms, a urine test can help determine whether protein is present.

### 2. Swelling in the Feet, Ankles or Around the Eyes

The kidneys help regulate the amount of salt and water in the body. If kidney function becomes impaired, excess fluid may sometimes accumulate.

This can lead to swelling, particularly around the:

- Feet

- Ankles

- Lower legs

- Hands

- Area around the eyes

Swelling has many possible causes, including heart, liver, venous and other medical conditions. It should therefore be assessed rather than automatically attributed to the kidneys.

### 3. Changes in Urination

Changes in urination can sometimes be an early clue that something needs to be investigated.

You might notice:

- Urinating more often than usual

- Urinating less than usual

- Needing to urinate repeatedly during the night

- Changes in the appearance of urine

- Difficulty passing urine

- A sudden change from your usual urinary pattern

These symptoms can also occur with urinary tract problems, prostate conditions, diabetes and other disorders. The important point is that a persistent change should not simply be ignored.

### 4. Blood in the Urine

Blood in urine, known medically as haematuria, should be evaluated.

The cause may be relatively straightforward, such as a urinary infection or kidney stone, but there are also other possible causes involving the kidneys or urinary tract.

Sometimes blood is visible. In other cases, it can only be detected through a urine test.

If you notice red, pink, brown or tea-coloured urine, particularly if this is new, speak with a healthcare professional.

### 5. Persistent or Unusual Fatigue

Feeling tired after a poor night's sleep or a demanding week is normal. Persistent fatigue that does not improve with adequate rest is different.

Advanced kidney dysfunction can contribute to tiredness through several mechanisms, including the effects of waste accumulation and anaemia associated with chronic kidney disease.

Fatigue is extremely common and has many possible causes, though. Thyroid disorders, anaemia, diabetes, sleep problems, infections, nutritional deficiencies and other medical conditions can all contribute.

The symptom becomes more useful when considered alongside the rest of the clinical picture.

### 6. Nausea or Reduced Appetite

As kidney function becomes significantly impaired, waste products can build up in the bloodstream. This may contribute to nausea, poor appetite or an unpleasant taste in the mouth.

These symptoms are not specific to kidney disease. Digestive conditions, infections, medications and many other problems can cause similar symptoms.

Persistent nausea or a noticeable loss of appetite should therefore be evaluated rather than self-diagnosed.

### 7. Itchy or Dry Skin

Persistent itching can occur in people with more advanced kidney disease, particularly when changes in mineral and waste-product balance occur.

But dry skin and itching are much more commonly associated with other conditions.

If itching is persistent, severe or unexplained, it is worth discussing with a doctor rather than assuming that the kidneys are responsible.

### 8. Muscle Cramps

Muscle cramps can have numerous causes, including dehydration, physical exertion, medication effects and changes in electrolyte levels.

Because the kidneys play an important role in maintaining electrolyte and fluid balance, significant kidney dysfunction can contribute to muscle symptoms.

Again, the symptom itself does not establish a diagnosis. Context and appropriate testing matter.

## Why Can Kidney Disease Be Difficult to Notice?

This is one of the most important things to understand about kidney health.

Early kidney disease may produce few or no obvious symptoms.

A person may feel completely well while blood or urine tests are already showing changes in kidney function. By the time noticeable symptoms develop, the underlying problem may have been present for some time.

This is particularly important for people with risk factors such as diabetes or high blood pressure.

For these individuals, routine medical follow-up may identify kidney problems before symptoms become obvious.

## Who Is at Higher Risk of Kidney Disease?

Certain health conditions and circumstances increase the likelihood of kidney problems.

### Diabetes

Diabetes is an important risk factor for chronic kidney disease. Persistently elevated blood glucose can damage the small blood vessels and filtering structures within the kidneys over time.

People with diabetes may therefore need periodic kidney checks even when they feel perfectly well.

### High Blood Pressure

High blood pressure can damage the blood vessels within the kidneys.

There is also a two-way relationship between kidney disease and hypertension. Kidney problems can contribute to high blood pressure, while uncontrolled high blood pressure can further damage the kidneys.

This makes blood pressure control an important part of long-term kidney care.

### Cardiovascular Disease

Heart and kidney health are closely connected. People with cardiovascular disease may have a higher risk of kidney problems, and kidney disease itself can increase cardiovascular risk.

### Family History

A family history of certain kidney diseases can increase an individual's risk.

This does not mean that a person with an affected family member will necessarily develop kidney disease. It simply means that the family history is relevant when assessing overall risk.

### Previous Kidney Problems

People who have previously experienced kidney injury, certain infections, kidney stones or other kidney-related problems may require closer attention depending on the circumstances.

### Increasing Age

Kidney function can change with age, although ageing alone should not be considered a disease.

Older adults may also have other conditions or take medications that make kidney monitoring particularly relevant.

## How Is Kidney Function Tested?

Symptoms are only one part of the assessment. Doctors commonly use blood and urine tests to look for evidence of kidney problems.

### Creatinine Blood Test

Creatinine is a waste product produced by normal muscle metabolism.

Healthy kidneys remove creatinine from the bloodstream. When kidney filtration decreases, blood creatinine levels can rise.

Creatinine is useful, but the number should not be interpreted in isolation.

### eGFR

The estimated glomerular filtration rate, or eGFR, uses creatinine along with other factors to estimate how effectively the kidneys are filtering blood.

It is one of the main measurements used when assessing kidney function.

A single eGFR result does not necessarily tell the entire story. Doctors usually consider the result alongside previous measurements, urine findings, medical history and other factors.

### Urine Testing

Urine tests can look for abnormalities such as:

- Protein

- Albumin

- Blood

- Signs of infection

- Other changes that may indicate kidney or urinary tract problems

An albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) is one commonly used urine measurement when assessing albumin leakage.

For someone at increased risk of kidney disease, both blood and urine testing can provide important information.

## What Does an Abnormal Kidney Test Mean?

An abnormal result does not automatically mean that a person has permanent kidney disease.

Kidney test results can be affected by several factors, and doctors generally interpret them in context.

For example, a temporary illness, dehydration, certain medications or an acute kidney problem can affect kidney-related blood tests.

This is why one abnormal result should not be interpreted without considering the clinical situation.

If a kidney test is abnormal, your doctor may recommend repeating the test, checking urine, reviewing medications, assessing blood pressure or investigating other possible causes.

## Can Diabetes Damage the Kidneys?

Yes. Diabetes is one of the important causes of chronic kidney disease.

High blood glucose over time can damage the tiny blood vessels and filtering structures inside the kidneys. The process can develop gradually, which is why a person with diabetes may not notice any kidney-related symptoms initially.

Regular monitoring can help identify changes earlier.

For people living with diabetes, kidney health is therefore not something that should only be checked when symptoms appear.

## Can High Blood Pressure Cause Kidney Disease?

Yes.

The kidneys contain a large network of small blood vessels. Persistently elevated blood pressure can place stress on these vessels and gradually contribute to kidney damage.

At the same time, kidney disease can make blood pressure more difficult to control.

This creates an important cycle: high blood pressure can damage the kidneys, and kidney disease can contribute to high blood pressure.

Keeping blood pressure under appropriate control is therefore an important part of protecting kidney health.

## Can Kidney Disease Cause Pain?

Sometimes, but not always.

Many forms of chronic kidney disease do not cause pain in the early stages.

Pain in the side or back may occur with conditions such as kidney stones, certain infections or other problems affecting the kidneys or urinary tract.

Severe pain, particularly when accompanied by fever, vomiting, blood in the urine or difficulty passing urine, should be medically assessed.

## What Other Conditions Can Cause Similar Symptoms?

One of the biggest mistakes people can make is assuming that a single symptom points to one particular disease.

For example:

Foamy urine can sometimes be related to protein in the urine, but occasional bubbles can occur for other reasons.

Swelling may occur with kidney disease but can also be associated with heart, liver, vascular and other conditions.

Fatigue can have dozens of possible causes.

Frequent urination can occur with diabetes, urinary infections, prostate problems, increased fluid intake and other conditions.

This is why symptoms should be interpreted as clues rather than diagnoses.

## When Should You See a Doctor About Kidney Symptoms?

Consider arranging a medical evaluation if you develop a persistent or unexplained change such as:

- Repeatedly foamy urine

- Blood in the urine

- New swelling

- Persistent fatigue

- A noticeable change in urination

- Recurrent urinary problems

- Unexplained nausea or loss of appetite

- Repeatedly elevated blood pressure

- Abnormal kidney blood or urine tests

You should also discuss kidney screening with your doctor if you have important risk factors such as diabetes, high blood pressure or a relevant family history.

The absence of symptoms does not necessarily mean that kidney function is normal.

## When Do Kidney Symptoms Require Urgent Medical Attention?

Some situations should not wait for a routine appointment.

Seek prompt medical attention if you experience symptoms such as:

:::emergency

- A major or sudden reduction in urine output

- Severe pain in the side or back

- Blood in the urine with significant symptoms

- Fever and chills with urinary or flank symptoms

- Severe vomiting with dehydration

- New severe swelling or breathing difficulty

- Confusion or significant changes in alertness

The appropriate response depends on the severity and combination of symptoms. If you are seriously unwell, seek urgent medical care.

:::

## Can Kidney Disease Be Prevented?

Not every form of kidney disease can be prevented. However, several measures can reduce risk and help protect kidney function.

#### Keep blood pressure under control

High blood pressure is an important risk factor for kidney damage. Regular monitoring and appropriate treatment can help reduce the strain on the kidneys.

#### Manage diabetes carefully

Good blood glucose management is an important part of protecting the kidneys in people with diabetes.

#### Stay appropriately hydrated

Normal hydration supports kidney function. However, people with certain heart, kidney or other medical conditions may have specific fluid requirements, so more water is not automatically better for everyone.

#### Be careful with medications

Some medicines can affect kidney function, particularly in certain circumstances or when used inappropriately.

Do not assume that an over-the-counter medicine is harmless simply because it does not require a prescription.

#### Attend recommended health checks

People at increased risk may benefit from periodic blood pressure, blood, urine and metabolic testing.

Early detection can make a difference because treatment can then focus on the underlying cause and on protecting remaining kidney function.

## Do Kidney Cleanses or Detoxes Actually Protect the Kidneys?

There is a lot of marketing around kidney cleanses, detox drinks and supplements.

The kidneys already perform the body's natural blood-filtration role. A commercial "kidney detox" is not a substitute for medical assessment or treatment.

Some supplements can also interact with medicines or may not be appropriate for people with kidney problems.

If you are concerned about your kidneys, a proper medical evaluation is much more useful than relying on a cleanse or detox product.

## What Is the Difference Between Acute Kidney Injury and Chronic Kidney Disease?

The time course is one of the major differences.

Acute kidney injury (AKI) develops over a relatively short period and can occur because of problems such as severe illness, dehydration, certain medications, obstruction or other medical conditions.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) involves abnormalities of kidney structure or function that persist over time.

The two conditions are not interchangeable.

A sudden abnormal kidney test should therefore be assessed carefully. It may represent an acute problem rather than permanent chronic kidney disease.

## What Happens If Kidney Disease Is Left Unchecked?

The consequences depend on the type, cause and severity of the kidney problem.

Some kidney conditions remain stable for long periods, while others can progressively worsen.

More advanced kidney disease can affect fluid balance, electrolyte levels, blood pressure, red blood cell production and the body's handling of waste products.

Kidney disease is also associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular complications.

This is why identifying a problem early and addressing the underlying cause is important.
