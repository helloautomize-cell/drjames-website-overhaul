Most people with [prediabetes](/conditions/diabetes-and-prediabetes/) feel completely normal. Energy is fine, weight has not changed much and thirst is nothing unusual. That is exactly why the condition slips past so many people. How you feel on an ordinary day says little about what your blood glucose is doing, and a blood test is the only way to find out.

For the wider picture, see our guide on [prediabetes early warning signs](/health-library/diabetes-and-prediabetes-early-warning-signs-you-shouldnt-ignore/).

## Why Prediabetes Often Feels Like Nothing

Prediabetes means blood glucose is higher than the healthy range but not yet high enough to meet the diagnosis of diabetes. At this stage, the body is usually coping. Early insulin resistance is offset by the pancreas producing more insulin, which keeps glucose low enough that daily life is not disrupted. The shift happens gradually, often over years, so there is rarely a moment when anything feels different.

## Common Myths About Feeling Fine

### "If I feel healthy, my blood sugar must be normal."

Symptoms generally appear only when glucose rises well above the prediabetes range. Good energy and no excess thirst are reassuring, but they cannot confirm normal glucose control.

### "Only overweight people get prediabetes."

Body weight is an important risk factor, but it is not the whole story. Family history, low physical activity, older age and a previous pregnancy complicated by gestational diabetes all raise risk. Some people at a healthy weight still develop prediabetes.

### "One normal result means I am safe for good."

A normal test is reassuring for the time it was taken. Changes in weight, blood pressure or medicines can shift risk over the following years, so repeat testing matters when risk factors are present.

### "Prediabetes does not matter because it is not diabetes."

Prediabetes is an early signal, and it is linked with a higher chance of developing type 2 diabetes. It also tends to travel with high blood pressure or abnormal cholesterol, both of which affect the heart and blood vessels over time.

## Warning Signs Worth Acting On

Prediabetes seldom announces itself, but a few clues should prompt a test:

- A parent or sibling with type 2 diabetes

- Dark, velvety skin on the neck, armpits or groin, which can reflect insulin resistance

- High blood pressure, or abnormal cholesterol or triglycerides

- Polycystic ovary syndrome, or gestational diabetes in an earlier pregnancy

- Increased thirst, frequent urination, unexplained weight loss, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds or repeated infections

No single sign confirms a diagnosis. The clues matter most when they cluster together or keep returning.

## When Should You Worry?

You do not need symptoms to ask for a test. If you have several of the risk factors above, booking a check is a reasonable step even when you feel well. If any of the warning signs persist, do not wait for them to settle on their own.

:::emergency

Some symptoms need urgent care. Very high blood sugar can cause vomiting or inability to keep fluids down, severe abdominal pain, rapid or deep breathing, drowsiness or confusion. These symptoms can point to a dangerous complication and should be assessed the same day.

:::

## What Should You Do Next?

Ask your doctor which test is most suitable for you, and bring your family history, your recent weight and blood pressure readings, and any history of diabetes during pregnancy. If the result is in the prediabetes range, the useful next step is a concrete plan. This usually means regular physical activity, gradual weight loss where it is appropriate, and a clear schedule for repeat testing. Some people at higher risk may be offered medication, and that decision depends on a full individual assessment rather than a number alone.

## Final Thoughts

Feeling well is a good sign, but it cannot tell you what your glucose is doing. A simple test can, and finding prediabetes early leaves more room to influence its course. If you are unsure whether you need testing, a short conversation with your doctor will usually settle the question.
