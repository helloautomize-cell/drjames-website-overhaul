There is a common assumption about diabetes: if something is wrong with your blood sugar, you will probably feel it.

That is not always the case.

A person can have [prediabetes](/conditions/diabetes-and-prediabetes/) and feel completely normal. There may be no unusual thirst, no obvious weight change and no dramatic change in energy levels. Sometimes the first indication is simply a blood test done during a routine health check.

This is what makes prediabetes easy to overlook.

Prediabetes means that blood glucose is higher than the normal range but has not reached the level used to diagnose diabetes. It does not mean that a person already has diabetes. It does, however, indicate that the body's ability to regulate blood sugar is changing and that the risk of developing type 2 diabetes is higher.

The good news is that finding prediabetes gives you something valuable: time to act.

Instead of waiting until symptoms become obvious, you can identify the problem, understand why it is happening and make changes that may reduce the chance of progressing to type 2 diabetes.

### What Is Prediabetes?

Prediabetes is a condition in which blood glucose is above the normal range but below the diagnostic range for diabetes.

One of the main processes involved is insulin resistance.

Insulin is a hormone that helps glucose move from the bloodstream into the body's cells, where it can be used for energy. When the body's tissues become less responsive to insulin, the pancreas has to work harder to maintain normal blood glucose levels.

For some time, the body may compensate by producing more insulin.

Eventually, that compensation may no longer be sufficient. Blood glucose starts to rise, and a person may move from normal glucose regulation into the prediabetes range and, in some cases, eventually into type 2 diabetes.

This progression is not identical for everyone.

Some people with prediabetes never develop diabetes, particularly when risk factors are addressed. Others may progress despite making changes, which is why follow-up remains important.

The important point is simple: prediabetes is a warning, not a diagnosis of inevitable diabetes.

## Can You Have Prediabetes Without Any Symptoms?

Yes.

In fact, this is one of the most important things to understand about prediabetes.

There is no single symptom that reliably tells you that you have prediabetes. Many people discover it only because their doctor orders an HbA1c, fasting blood glucose or another glucose test.

This is why searching for a list of “prediabetes symptoms” can sometimes create confusion.

The condition itself may be silent.

As blood glucose rises further, however, symptoms associated with hyperglycemia may become noticeable. Increased thirst, frequent urination, unexplained weight loss, fatigue and blurred vision can occur with diabetes and significant high blood sugar.

But symptoms are not a substitute for testing.

You can feel perfectly healthy and still have an abnormal HbA1c or fasting glucose result.

That is particularly relevant if you have a family history of diabetes, excess weight, high blood pressure, a history of gestational diabetes or other risk factors.

## What Are the Early Warning Signs of Diabetes?

Prediabetes often has no obvious warning signs. Diabetes, particularly when blood glucose becomes significantly elevated, is more likely to cause symptoms.

Possible symptoms include:

- Feeling unusually thirsty

- Passing urine more frequently than usual

- Feeling hungry much of the time

- Losing weight without trying

- Feeling unusually tired

- Blurred vision

- Cuts or wounds taking longer to heal

- Repeated infections

- General weakness

There is an important caveat here.

None of these symptoms, by themselves, proves that a person has diabetes.

Fatigue, for example, is extremely common and can have many causes. Frequent urination can also occur for reasons that have nothing to do with blood glucose.

The symptoms become more meaningful when they occur together, persist or appear alongside abnormal blood glucose results.

#### Prediabetes and diabetes are not the same thing

This distinction is worth remembering.

Prediabetes: often produces no noticeable symptoms.

Diabetes with significant hyperglycemia: may produce symptoms such as excessive thirst, frequent urination and unexplained weight loss.

So if you are waiting for symptoms before getting tested, you may be waiting too long.

## Why Does Prediabetes Often Go Unnoticed?

Prediabetes usually develops gradually.

Blood glucose does not necessarily jump from normal to very high overnight. The body may compensate for insulin resistance for quite some time, keeping glucose within a range that does not produce obvious symptoms.

This allows the condition to progress quietly.

A person may therefore say:

“I feel completely fine. Why would I need a diabetes test?”

The answer depends on the person's risk profile.

Screening is not based only on symptoms. Age, family history, body weight, physical activity, blood pressure, previous pregnancy-related diabetes and other factors can all influence whether testing is appropriate.

Current American Diabetes Association guidance recommends screening according to age and risk factors rather than waiting for symptoms to appear.

## What Causes Prediabetes?

There isn't one single cause.

Prediabetes usually develops through a combination of genetic, metabolic and lifestyle factors. Some of these can be modified; others cannot.

### Insulin resistance

Insulin resistance is one of the central mechanisms behind prediabetes.

When cells respond less effectively to insulin, the body has to produce more insulin to keep glucose under control. Over time, the pancreas may struggle to compensate adequately.

That is when blood glucose can begin to rise.

### Excess body weight

Overweight and obesity increase the risk of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.

However, it would be inaccurate to say that diabetes only affects people who are overweight.

People at different body weights can develop prediabetes or diabetes.

Weight is one factor among several.

### Family history

Your genes matter.

Having a parent, brother or sister with type 2 diabetes increases your risk. This is one reason family history is routinely considered when deciding who may benefit from screening.

### Physical inactivity

The body uses glucose differently when you are physically active.

Regular movement helps improve insulin sensitivity and supports metabolic health. Long periods of inactivity can contribute to insulin resistance, particularly when combined with other risk factors.

### Age

The likelihood of developing prediabetes and type 2 diabetes generally increases with age.

Current ADA guidance recommends diabetes screening for adults beginning at age 35, with earlier screening considered when other risk factors are present.

### Previous gestational diabetes

Women who have had gestational diabetes have a higher long-term risk of developing type 2 diabetes.

This is one reason follow-up after pregnancy matters, even if blood glucose returned to normal after delivery.

### Other health conditions

High blood pressure, abnormal cholesterol or triglyceride levels, cardiovascular disease and certain other metabolic conditions can occur alongside increased diabetes risk.

Taken together, these factors provide a much clearer picture than symptoms alone.

## How Is Prediabetes Diagnosed?

Prediabetes is diagnosed using blood tests.

The three commonly used tests are:

1. HbA1c

2. Fasting plasma glucose

3. Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)

Each test looks at glucose regulation in a slightly different way.

That means two people with similar health profiles may not necessarily have identical results across all three tests.

## What Blood Sugar Levels Indicate Prediabetes?

For nonpregnant individuals, the commonly used diagnostic ranges are:

| Test | Normal Range | Prediabetes Range | Diabetes Range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HbA1c | Below 5.7% | 5.7 to 6.4% | 6.5% or higher |
| Fasting plasma glucose | Below 100 mg/dL | 100 to 125 mg/dL | 126 mg/dL or higher |
| 2-hour OGTT | Below 140 mg/dL | 140 to 199 mg/dL | 200 mg/dL or higher |

These numbers should not be interpreted in isolation.

A diagnosis of diabetes may require confirmation, particularly when there are no clear symptoms of hyperglycemia. Your doctor may repeat a test or use another diagnostic test depending on the circumstances.

## What Does HbA1c Tell You?

HbA1c is one of the most useful tests for assessing long-term glucose control.

Unlike a fasting glucose test, it does not normally require you to fast. Instead, it reflects the amount of glucose attached to hemoglobin and provides an estimate of average blood glucose over approximately the previous two to three months.

An HbA1c result of:

- Below 5.7% is generally considered normal

- 5.7 to 6.4% falls in the prediabetes range

- 6.5% or higher falls in the diabetes diagnostic range

There are situations in which HbA1c may not accurately reflect blood glucose.

Certain blood disorders, conditions affecting red blood cells and some hemoglobin variants can interfere with the result. Your doctor may therefore recommend another glucose test if the HbA1c result does not fit the rest of the clinical picture.

## What Does a Fasting Blood Sugar Test Show?

Fasting plasma glucose measures blood glucose after fasting.

A result between 100 and 125 mg/dL is within the prediabetes range.

A fasting glucose result of 126 mg/dL or higher is within the diabetes diagnostic range, subject to appropriate confirmation when required.

One abnormal result does not always tell the complete story.

For example, your doctor may want to repeat the test or compare it with HbA1c depending on your symptoms, previous results and overall risk.

## What Is an Oral Glucose Tolerance Test?

The oral glucose tolerance test, commonly called an OGTT, examines how your body handles a measured amount of glucose.

After fasting, you drink a glucose-containing solution. Blood glucose is then measured at a specified time, commonly two hours later.

A two-hour result of:

- 140 to 199 mg/dL is within the prediabetes range

- 200 mg/dL or higher meets the glucose criterion for diabetes

The OGTT can identify glucose abnormalities that may not be obvious from fasting glucose alone.

It is not used as routinely as HbA1c or fasting glucose in every situation, but it can be useful when the diagnosis remains uncertain or when a more detailed assessment of glucose tolerance is needed.

## Who Should Get Tested for Prediabetes?

You do not need to have symptoms before considering diabetes screening.

Testing may be particularly relevant if you have one or more risk factors such as:

- A parent, brother or sister with type 2 diabetes

- Overweight or obesity

- High blood pressure

- Abnormal cholesterol or triglyceride levels

- Cardiovascular disease

- A history of gestational diabetes

- Polycystic ovary syndrome

- Previous abnormal blood glucose

- Limited physical activity

- Increasing age

- Other conditions associated with insulin resistance

The appropriate screening interval depends on your individual risk.

For people who already have prediabetes, annual testing is generally recommended.

If your blood sugar is normal, testing may still need to be repeated periodically because risk can change with age, weight, medications and other health conditions.

## Can Prediabetes Be Reversed?

This is one of the first questions many people ask after receiving a prediabetes result.

The answer is encouraging, but it needs to be explained carefully.

Blood glucose can return to the normal range in some people after changes in diet, physical activity, weight and other lifestyle factors.

That does not necessarily mean the underlying tendency toward diabetes has disappeared forever.

Think of it as reducing risk rather than receiving a permanent “all clear.”

Maintaining the changes that helped improve blood glucose remains important.

For people with overweight, even a modest reduction in body weight can have metabolic benefits. Diabetes prevention programs also emphasize regular physical activity.

## What Can You Do If You Have Prediabetes?

You don't need to completely redesign your life overnight.

Small changes that you can maintain are often more useful than an extreme plan that lasts for a few weeks.

### Increase physical activity

Regular movement helps the body use glucose more effectively.

Walking is a practical starting point for many people.

A commonly used target is at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity per week, although the appropriate level depends on your health and fitness.

If you have been inactive for a long time or have another medical condition, speak with your doctor before starting a demanding exercise program.

### Look at your overall diet

Prediabetes does not mean that you can never eat rice, roti or other carbohydrate-containing foods.

The bigger issue is often the overall eating pattern.

For an Indian diet, it may be useful to look at:

- Portion sizes

- Sugary drinks

- Sweets and desserts

- Refined carbohydrates

- Highly processed snacks

- Vegetable intake

- Protein sources

- Fibre-rich foods

- Frequency of eating outside the home

The aim is not necessarily to eliminate one food.

It is to build meals that support better glucose control and are realistic enough to follow over the long term.

### If appropriate, work toward a healthier weight

For people with overweight, modest weight loss can improve insulin sensitivity and metabolic health.

There is no universal “ideal” rate of weight loss.

A sustainable approach is generally more useful than trying to lose weight as quickly as possible.

### Pay attention to sleep

Sleep is often overlooked when people talk about blood sugar.

Poor sleep can affect appetite, activity, weight and metabolic health.

If you regularly snore heavily, wake up feeling exhausted or have other symptoms that suggest sleep apnea, mention them to your doctor.

### Keep up with follow-up testing

Improving your HbA1c or fasting glucose is good news.

It is not, however, a reason to stop paying attention to your health.

Prediabetes can return or progress over time, so appropriate monitoring remains important.

## What Happens If Prediabetes Is Ignored?

Not everyone with prediabetes develops diabetes.

But the condition does increase the risk of eventually developing type 2 diabetes.

It is also associated with broader cardiometabolic risk.

Ignoring a prediabetes result therefore means missing an opportunity to identify and address risk factors early.

On the other hand, there is no need to panic over a prediabetes diagnosis.

The result is information.

It tells you that your glucose regulation deserves attention and gives you an opportunity to discuss what should happen next with your doctor.

## Prediabetes vs Diabetes: What's the Difference?

The simplest distinction is the blood glucose range.

Prediabetes is between normal glucose regulation and diabetes.

For example:

HbA1c

- Normal: below 5.7%

- Prediabetes: 5.7 to 6.4%

- Diabetes: 6.5% or higher

Fasting plasma glucose

- Normal: below 100 mg/dL

- Prediabetes: 100 to 125 mg/dL

- Diabetes: 126 mg/dL or higher

Two-hour OGTT

- Normal: below 140 mg/dL

- Prediabetes: 140 to 199 mg/dL

- Diabetes: 200 mg/dL or higher

These numbers are useful, but diagnosis is more than simply comparing one result with a table.

Your doctor will consider the test used, whether you were symptomatic, whether the result needs confirmation and whether there are factors that could affect the accuracy of the test.

## Common Myths About Prediabetes

### “If I feel fine, my blood sugar must be fine.”

Not necessarily.

Prediabetes often has no noticeable symptoms. A blood test is much more reliable for detecting it than the way you feel on a particular day.

### “Prediabetes means I already have diabetes.”

No.

Prediabetes and diabetes are different diagnostic categories.

Prediabetes means glucose is higher than normal but below the diabetes threshold.

It does, however, mean that your future risk deserves attention.

### “Only overweight people develop diabetes.”

No.

Excess weight is an important risk factor, but it is not the only one.

Family history, age, physical activity, previous gestational diabetes and other metabolic factors also matter.

### “I should wait until I have symptoms before getting tested.”

This can be a mistake.

People with significant risk factors may need screening even when they feel completely well.

### “If my blood sugar becomes normal, the problem is permanently gone.”

Not necessarily.

A healthier glucose result is encouraging, but the underlying risk can remain.

Continuing healthy habits and following the recommended testing schedule are still important.

## When Should You See an Internal Medicine Doctor?

Consider speaking with an Internal Medicine physician if:

- You have been told that your blood sugar is borderline.

- Your HbA1c is in the prediabetes range.

- You have a strong family history of diabetes.

- You have high blood pressure or abnormal cholesterol.

- You have overweight or obesity together with other risk factors.

- You previously had gestational diabetes.

- You are experiencing increased thirst or frequent urination.

- You have unexplained weight loss.

- You have received different blood sugar results and aren't sure what they mean.

- You want to understand your future diabetes risk even though you currently feel well.

An Internal Medicine consultation can look beyond the glucose number.

Blood pressure, cholesterol, weight, family history, medications, lifestyle, sleep and other health conditions can all form part of the assessment.

That broader view is particularly useful because diabetes rarely exists in isolation.

## When Can High Blood Sugar Become an Emergency?

Most people with mildly elevated blood glucose do not have a medical emergency.

However, very high blood sugar accompanied by severe symptoms requires prompt medical attention.

:::emergency

Seek urgent medical care if you experience symptoms such as:

- Severe vomiting

- Significant dehydration

- Confusion or altered consciousness

- Difficulty breathing

- Severe weakness

- Significant abdominal pain

- Loss of consciousness

- Very high blood glucose together with concerning symptoms

:::

Severe hyperglycemia can sometimes lead to acute metabolic complications that require hospital treatment.

If you are seriously unwell, don't wait for a routine appointment.

## The Most Important Thing to Remember About Prediabetes

Perhaps the most important fact about prediabetes is also the easiest to overlook:

You may have it without feeling different at all.

That is why symptoms should not be the only reason to think about diabetes screening.

If you have risk factors, testing can identify abnormal glucose before you develop obvious symptoms.

And if your results show prediabetes, you still have an opportunity to do something about it.

Improving physical activity, reviewing your eating pattern, addressing excess weight when appropriate and keeping up with medical follow-up can all play a role in reducing future risk.

Prediabetes is a warning.

It is not a prediction that you will definitely develop diabetes.

The earlier you understand what your numbers mean, the more useful that information can become.
