If your readings have come back higher than you would like, you probably want to know what you can change yourself. Quite a lot, as it turns out. Diet, activity, body weight, alcohol, smoking and sleep all push blood pressure up or down. For people already on medicine, the same habits can make treatment work better and protect the heart and kidneys over the long term.

For the wider picture, see our guide on [Understanding High Blood Pressure: Causes, Risks & Management](/health-library/understanding-high-blood-pressure-causes-risks-and-management/).

## Will Lifestyle Changes Really Bring Blood Pressure Down?

Often they make a measurable difference, though people respond differently. How far a reading drops depends on your starting point, your age and your other risk factors. Some people reach their target through lifestyle changes alone. Others need them alongside tablets. The only reliable way to find out is to track home readings for several weeks and compare averages, not single numbers.

## Step-by-Step Lifestyle Plan

### Step 1: Get a baseline first

You cannot judge progress without a starting point. Sit quietly for five minutes with your back supported and feet flat. Rest your bare arm on a table at heart level, take two readings about a minute apart, and write both down. Your doctor can check that your monitor and cuff size are right.

### Step 2: Trace the salt

The World Health Organization advises adults to eat less than 5 grams of salt a day, roughly one teaspoon. Most of it does not come from the salt shaker. Bread, sauces, stock cubes, processed meat, crisps and restaurant dishes carry much of the load. Compare sodium figures on labels, and use garlic, lemon, herbs and pepper to keep food flavourful.

### Step 3: Change the plate, not the whole menu

Build meals around vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans, lentils, nuts and fish. The DASH eating plan was designed for blood pressure and makes a sensible template. Fried food, sugary drinks and heavily processed meals are usually the first things to cut back. Swapping one meal a day is easier to keep up than an overnight overhaul.

### Step 4: Work up to 150 minutes of movement

WHO guidance suggests at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, which for most people means about 30 minutes on five days. Brisk walking, cycling and swimming all count, and two strength sessions a week add more. If you have been inactive, start with ten-minute walks and build up. Anyone with heart disease or joint problems should check their plan with a doctor first.

### Step 5: Lose weight at a steady pace

Extra weight raises blood pressure, and even a modest loss can help. Slow, steady loss over months is more likely to last than a crash diet, and it is gentler on the body.

### Step 6: Be honest about alcohol

Regular heavy drinking pushes blood pressure up in a clear way. For some people, cutting back or taking a few weeks off makes a visible difference. Ask your doctor what amount is reasonable for you.

### Step 7: Stop smoking

Smoking narrows and damages blood vessels, adding to the risk of heart attack and stroke. Quitting lowers that risk even if blood pressure itself falls slowly. Your doctor can suggest support, including medicines or counselling where appropriate.

### Step 8: Pay attention to your sleep

Loud snoring, pauses in breathing, waking up gasping or feeling exhausted after a full night can point to sleep apnea. Sleep apnea is closely linked to [high blood pressure](/conditions/high-blood-pressure/), and treating it can make readings easier to control. Mention these signs to your doctor.

### Step 9: Make room to unwind

Breathing exercises, time outdoors and a steady bedtime routine can help some people feel calmer. These habits support the rest of the plan, but they are not a substitute for it.

## What Should You Do Now?

Start small. Pick two changes you can realistically keep for the next month, such as swapping packaged snacks for fruit and walking after dinner. Keep recording your home readings and bring the log to your next visit. If you take medicine, keep taking it. Better numbers mean the plan is working, and any change to treatment should come from your doctor.

## When Should You See a Doctor?

:::emergency

Book a review if your readings stay high after several weeks of consistent effort, or if you have diabetes or kidney disease. Seek emergency care straight away for a severe headache, chest pain, breathlessness, confusion, sudden weakness or numbness on one side, slurred speech or sudden changes in vision.

:::
