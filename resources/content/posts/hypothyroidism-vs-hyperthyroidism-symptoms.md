Someone with hyperthyroidism may instead experience unexplained weight loss, heat intolerance, sweating, tremor, anxiety, a fast or irregular heartbeat and more frequent bowel movements. There is considerable overlap, however. For example, both conditions can cause fatigue, menstrual changes and difficulty concentrating. The symptoms alone cannot tell you which thyroid disorder you have. Blood tests such as TSH and free T4, with additional testing when appropriate, are used to investigate thyroid function.

Feeling unusually tired? Gaining or losing weight without making major changes to your diet? Perhaps your heart has started racing, you are more sensitive to heat or cold, or you simply don't feel like yourself.

These symptoms can make people wonder whether their thyroid is responsible.

The thyroid is a small gland in the front of the neck, but the hormones it produces influence many parts of the body. They help regulate how quickly the body uses energy and affect the heart, muscles, digestive system, temperature regulation and several other functions.

Two of the most common thyroid problems are hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism.

They are, in broad terms, opposite problems:

- Hypothyroidism means the thyroid does not produce enough thyroid hormone.

- Hyperthyroidism means the body is exposed to too much thyroid hormone.

The difficulty is that symptoms do not always follow a neat checklist. Fatigue, changes in weight, sleep problems and changes in mood can occur with either condition, and can also be caused by many problems unrelated to the thyroid.

That is why symptoms can raise suspicion, but thyroid blood tests are needed to establish the diagnosis.

### What Does the Thyroid Gland Actually Do?

The thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland located at the front of the neck.

Its main job is to produce thyroid hormones, particularly thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). These hormones influence the rate at which many processes in the body take place.

The thyroid does not work independently.

It is part of a regulatory system involving the hypothalamus and pituitary gland in the brain. The pituitary produces thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), which signals the thyroid to make thyroid hormones.

This relationship is important when interpreting blood tests.

When thyroid hormone levels fall, the pituitary generally increases TSH production. When thyroid hormone levels rise, TSH production usually falls.

That is why TSH is commonly used as an initial test when a thyroid problem is suspected.

## What Is Hypothyroidism?

Hypothyroidism is an underactive thyroid.

In this condition, the thyroid does not make enough thyroid hormone for the body's needs.

The change can happen gradually. Some people notice several symptoms developing over months rather than suddenly waking up with an obvious thyroid problem.

This gradual onset is one reason hypothyroidism can sometimes be mistaken for stress, lack of sleep, ageing or other health problems.

#### Common Causes of Hypothyroidism

One of the most common causes is Hashimoto's disease, an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks thyroid tissue.

Other possible causes include:

- Thyroiditis

- Previous thyroid surgery

- Certain types of radiation treatment

- Some medicines

- Problems involving the pituitary or hypothalamus

- Certain iodine-related conditions

The cause matters because treatment and follow-up may differ depending on what has affected the thyroid.

### What Are the Symptoms of Hypothyroidism?

Hypothyroidism can affect several systems at once.

Common symptoms include:

- Persistent tiredness

- Feeling unusually cold

- Weight gain

- Dry skin

- Dry or thinning hair

- Constipation

- Muscle aches or weakness

- Joint discomfort

- Slower heart rate

- Low mood

- Difficulty concentrating

- Heavier or irregular menstrual periods

- Fertility problems in some people

Not everyone develops all of these symptoms.

In fact, someone may have only a few noticeable changes, particularly during the early stages.

That is why it is better to look at the overall pattern rather than trying to match one symptom to a thyroid disorder.

## What Is Hyperthyroidism?

Hyperthyroidism is an overactive thyroid.

Here, the thyroid produces too much thyroid hormone, causing many body processes to become more active.

A person may feel as though their body is constantly running faster than usual.

#### Common Causes of Hyperthyroidism

Graves' disease is a common cause. It is an autoimmune disorder that stimulates the thyroid to produce excess hormone.

Other causes include:

- Overactive thyroid nodules

- Thyroiditis

- Excess iodine in certain circumstances

- Taking too much thyroid hormone medicine

- Rarely, problems involving the pituitary gland

Again, identifying the cause is important because treatment is not identical for every patient.

### What Are the Symptoms of Hyperthyroidism?

Typical symptoms can include:

- Unintentional weight loss

- Feeling unusually hot

- Increased sweating

- Fast or irregular heartbeat

- Shaking or trembling

- Nervousness or irritability

- Difficulty sleeping

- Muscle weakness

- Fatigue

- More frequent bowel movements

- Increased appetite

- Menstrual changes

- Problems with fertility

- Enlargement of the thyroid gland, known as a goitre

Some people, particularly older adults, may not develop the classic combination of symptoms.

A rapid or irregular heartbeat can be particularly important because untreated hyperthyroidism can affect cardiovascular health.

## Hypothyroidism vs. Hyperthyroidism: What's the Difference?

| Feature | Hypothyroidism | Hyperthyroidism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thyroid activity | Too little hormone production | Too much thyroid hormone |
| Weight | Weight gain may occur | Weight loss may occur |
| Temperature | Often feels cold | Often feels hot |
| Heart rate | May be slower | May be faster or irregular |
| Energy | Tiredness and sluggishness | Restlessness, weakness or fatigue |
| Bowel habits | Constipation | More frequent bowel movements |
| Skin | Dry skin | Warm, moist or sweaty skin |
| Sleep | Can involve excessive sleepiness | Difficulty sleeping is common |
| Mood | Low mood can occur | Anxiety or irritability can occur |
| Tremor | Less typical | More typical |
| Appetite | May decrease | May increase |
| Menstrual cycle | Can become heavier or irregular | Can become lighter or irregular |

This table is useful as a general comparison, but it should not be used as a diagnostic test.

Symptoms overlap, and other medical conditions can produce the same complaints.

## Why Can Thyroid Symptoms Be So Confusing?

This is one of the most common sources of confusion.

Imagine two people who both complain of being exhausted.

One may have hypothyroidism.

Another may have hyperthyroidism.

That might sound contradictory, but it is possible.

With hyperthyroidism, the body is working at an accelerated rate. Persistent sleep disturbance, increased metabolic activity and muscle weakness can eventually leave a person exhausted.

With hypothyroidism, reduced thyroid hormone activity can contribute directly to sluggishness and fatigue.

The same symptom can therefore have very different underlying causes.

The same applies to changes in mood, concentration and weight.

Symptoms are clues. They are not proof.

## Is Weight Gain Always a Sign of Hypothyroidism?

No.

Weight gain can occur with hypothyroidism, but it does not automatically mean someone has an underactive thyroid.

Body weight is influenced by:

- Diet

- Physical activity

- Sleep

- Medications

- Age

- Hormonal factors

- Metabolic conditions

- Fluid retention

- Other health conditions

Likewise, unexplained weight loss does not automatically mean hyperthyroidism.

If weight changes are persistent or unexplained, it is worth discussing them with a doctor rather than assuming the thyroid is responsible.

## Can Anxiety Be Caused by a Thyroid Problem?

It can.

Hyperthyroidism may produce symptoms that resemble anxiety, including:

- Nervousness

- Restlessness

- Trembling

- Palpitations

- Difficulty sleeping

- Irritability

This can sometimes lead a person to focus entirely on stress or anxiety when an underlying thyroid problem also needs to be considered.

On the other hand, having anxiety does not mean that a person has hyperthyroidism.

A proper assessment is needed, particularly when anxiety-like symptoms occur alongside unexplained weight loss, sweating, tremor or a persistently fast heartbeat.

## How Are Thyroid Disorders Diagnosed?

A thyroid disorder cannot reliably be diagnosed from symptoms alone.

A doctor usually considers the symptoms and medical history first, followed by an examination and appropriate blood tests.

### TSH Test

TSH, or thyroid-stimulating hormone, is commonly the first thyroid blood test.

In many cases:

- A high TSH may indicate an underactive thyroid.

- A low TSH may indicate an overactive thyroid.

But the result needs to be interpreted alongside other thyroid hormone levels and the clinical situation.

### Free T4 Test

Free T4 measures the available thyroxine circulating in the blood.

A pattern of high TSH with low free T4 commonly indicates primary hypothyroidism.

A low TSH together with an elevated free T4 commonly points toward hyperthyroidism.

There are exceptions and more complicated situations, which is why interpreting one laboratory value in isolation can be misleading.

### T3 Test

T3 testing can be particularly useful when hyperthyroidism is suspected.

In some people with hyperthyroidism, T3 may be elevated even when T4 is not clearly elevated.

T3 is generally less useful as a routine test for diagnosing straightforward hypothyroidism.

### Thyroid Antibody Tests

Antibody testing may help determine whether an autoimmune condition is involved.

For example, thyroid antibodies can support the diagnosis of conditions such as:

- Hashimoto's thyroiditis

- Graves' disease

The exact antibody test used depends on the clinical question.

### Does Everyone With a Thyroid Problem Need an Ultrasound?

No.

A thyroid ultrasound looks primarily at the structure of the thyroid, including nodules and other physical abnormalities.

It is different from a blood test, which evaluates thyroid function.

Depending on the situation, doctors may also use other imaging or thyroid uptake studies to investigate why thyroid hormone levels are abnormal.

## What Happens After a Thyroid Problem Is Diagnosed?

Treatment depends on the specific disorder, its cause, the severity of the abnormality and the person's overall health.

There is no single treatment that applies to every thyroid patient.

### Treatment for Hypothyroidism

The standard treatment for many people with established hypothyroidism is levothyroxine, a synthetic form of thyroid hormone.

The aim is to replace the hormone the thyroid is no longer producing adequately.

Treatment is monitored with blood tests, and the dose may need adjustment over time.

It is important not to increase, reduce or stop thyroid medication without discussing it with the treating clinician.

Taking more thyroid hormone than the body needs can also cause problems, including effects on the heart and bones.

## How Is Hyperthyroidism Treated?

Treatment depends heavily on the cause.

Possible approaches include:

- Medicines that reduce thyroid hormone production

- Medicines such as beta blockers to help control certain symptoms

- Radioactive iodine treatment

- Surgery in selected situations

Not every patient needs the same approach.

For example, treatment decisions can be different for Graves' disease, thyroiditis and an overactive thyroid nodule.

The goal is not simply to make a laboratory number normal. The underlying reason for the abnormal thyroid function needs to be considered as well.

## Can Thyroid Problems Be Managed Without Medication?

This depends entirely on the type of thyroid disorder.

Some temporary thyroid problems may resolve or change over time.

However, established hypothyroidism often requires thyroid hormone replacement.

Similarly, untreated hyperthyroidism should not simply be ignored because the symptoms seem manageable.

Persistent excess thyroid hormone can affect the heart, bones and other parts of the body.

The safest approach is to identify the cause first and then decide whether treatment is necessary.

## What About Diet and Thyroid Health?

Diet is important for overall health, but there is no universal “thyroid diet” that replaces appropriate medical treatment.

Iodine deserves particular attention.

The thyroid needs iodine to make thyroid hormones, but more iodine is not necessarily better. In some people, excessive iodine intake can worsen certain [thyroid disorders](/conditions/thyroid-disorders/).

Be cautious with supplements marketed specifically for thyroid health.

“Natural” does not automatically mean safe, particularly when a product contains iodine, thyroid-active ingredients or multiple herbal compounds.

If you have a diagnosed thyroid condition, discuss supplements with your doctor before starting them.

## Thyroid Disorders, Pregnancy and Fertility

Thyroid function can be particularly important when someone is trying to conceive, is pregnant or is experiencing fertility problems.

Both underactive and overactive thyroid conditions can affect menstrual cycles and fertility.

Thyroid disorders during pregnancy also require appropriate medical attention because thyroid hormones play an important role in maternal health and fetal development.

Anyone who has a known thyroid disorder and is planning pregnancy should discuss thyroid management with their healthcare professional.

## When Should You See a Doctor About Thyroid Symptoms?

You do not need to wait until symptoms become severe.

Consider speaking with a doctor if you have persistent or unexplained:

- Fatigue

- Weight change

- Heat or cold intolerance

- Palpitations

- Tremor

- Constipation

- Frequent bowel movements

- Hair or skin changes

- Menstrual changes

- Anxiety or irritability

- Difficulty concentrating

- Neck swelling

A doctor can decide whether thyroid testing is appropriate or whether another explanation should be investigated.

## When Is a Thyroid Problem Urgent?

Most thyroid symptoms do not require emergency treatment.

However, some symptoms deserve prompt medical assessment.

:::emergency

Seek urgent medical attention if you develop severe chest pain, significant difficulty breathing, fainting, severe confusion, or a very fast or irregular heartbeat accompanied by feeling seriously unwell.

:::

Severe untreated thyroid hormone disturbances can occasionally lead to serious complications, so significant deterioration should not be ignored.

## Common Myths About Hypothyroidism and Hyperthyroidism

### Myth: “If I'm tired, I must have hypothyroidism.”

Fact: Fatigue is common but nonspecific. Sleep problems, anaemia, infections, nutritional deficiencies, medications, stress and many other conditions can cause tiredness.

### Myth: “Weight gain always means an underactive thyroid.”

Fact: Hypothyroidism can contribute to weight gain, but it is only one possible cause.

### Myth: “Anxiety means I have hyperthyroidism.”

Fact: Hyperthyroidism can produce anxiety-like symptoms, but anxiety has many possible causes.

### Myth: “A normal thyroid test means nothing is wrong with me.”

Fact: Normal thyroid tests make a thyroid disorder less likely in many situations, but they do not mean every possible cause of your symptoms has been ruled out.

Persistent symptoms may require a broader medical evaluation.

### Myth: “Thyroid medicine is something I can adjust myself.”

Fact: Thyroid medication affects hormone levels, and too little or too much treatment can cause problems. Dose changes should be made with appropriate medical guidance.
