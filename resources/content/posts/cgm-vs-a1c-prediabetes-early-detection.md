Many patients now ask about continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) after seeing them on social media. Some companies sell these sensors to people without diabetes, and the marketing can make them sound like a better version of a blood test. They are not. A1C and CGMs measure related things, but they answer different questions, and the right choice depends on what you are trying to find out.

For the wider picture, see our guide on [early warning signs of prediabetes](/health-library/diabetes-and-prediabetes-early-warning-signs-you-shouldnt-ignore/).

## What Does an A1C Test Measure?

A1C estimates your average blood glucose over the past two to three months. It requires one blood sample, and fasting is not needed.

Below 5.7% is considered normal. A result from 5.7% to 6.4% falls in the [prediabetes](/conditions/diabetes-and-prediabetes/) range, and 6.5% or higher is in the diabetes range.

Because it is an average, two people can have the same A1C and very different days. One may spike sharply after lunch and then settle back to normal. The other may stay fairly even all day. A1C alone cannot distinguish them.

A1C can also mislead in certain people. Anemia, recent blood loss or transfusion, kidney disease, pregnancy and inherited hemoglobin disorders such as thalassemia can all shift the reading. Thalassemia is found in parts of the Gulf region, so if you know you have a blood condition, mention it before your doctor relies on A1C alone.

## What Does a CGM Show?

A CGM is a small sensor worn on the upper arm or abdomen. It measures glucose in the fluid beneath the skin every few minutes, and most sensors stay in place for 10 to 15 days.

Rather than one number, you get a daily pattern. A CGM can show:

- How high glucose climbs after breakfast, lunch or dinner

- How many hours a day glucose stays above the usual range

- Lows overnight or in the early morning

- Whether poor sleep, stress or specific meals line up with spikes

Glucose can rise after eating well before the three-month average changes. This is why a CGM sometimes shows early shifts that the A1C has not yet picked up.

## Can a CGM Diagnose Prediabetes?

No. Diagnosis still rests on A1C, fasting plasma glucose or the oral glucose tolerance test. The American Diabetes Association guidance I reviewed still uses these tests for diagnosis. CGMs are most firmly established as a tool for managing diabetes.

Consumer CGMs have limits too. The sensor measures interstitial fluid, which lags behind blood glucose by several minutes. Accuracy in people without diabetes has been studied less thoroughly. A single high reading after a meal can also cause needless worry, and it often says less than the overall pattern does.

## Which Test Finds Prediabetes Earlier?

For a diagnosis, A1C or fasting glucose is the reliable choice. For spotting early trouble with blood sugar control, a CGM may reveal patterns that a blood test misses.

The two complement each other. A normal A1C alongside frequent large spikes after meals is a good reason to review your diet and activity, and to ask your doctor whether further testing makes sense.

## What Should You Do Next?

If you have risk factors, such as a parent or sibling with type 2 diabetes, excess weight, high blood pressure, gestational diabetes in a past pregnancy or polycystic ovary syndrome, ask your doctor about glucose testing now. Waiting for symptoms is the wrong trigger.

If you are thinking about a CGM, bring the readings to your appointment. Do not start, stop or adjust diabetes medicine based on sensor data alone.

## When Should You See a Doctor?

See a doctor soon if you notice increased thirst, frequent urination, weight loss you cannot explain, blurred vision or unusual tiredness.

:::emergency

Seek urgent care if you develop confusion, drowsiness, persistent vomiting, severe belly pain or difficulty breathing, particularly alongside very high glucose readings.

:::
