After a high reading, most patients ask the same follow-up question: who should be looking after this? For the majority of people, the answer is a primary care doctor or internist. They can confirm the diagnosis, start treatment, adjust medicines and check the kidneys, blood sugar and cholesterol. A cardiologist tends to join the team in more specific situations, such as pressure that won't settle on treatment or existing heart disease. Plenty of patients end up seeing both, each for a different part of the picture.

For the wider picture, see our guide on [high blood pressure causes, risks and management](/health-library/understanding-high-blood-pressure-causes-risks-and-management/).

## Why Primary Care Is Usually the Starting Point

Hypertension is a long-term condition, so the care is mostly ongoing review.

Your primary doctor will typically repeat readings to confirm the diagnosis, order routine blood and urine tests, and talk through diet, activity and weight. They choose medicines, watch for side effects and make changes when needed. They also look at the wider health picture, because blood pressure is rarely a problem on its own. Sleep, blood sugar, kidney function and certain over-the-counter drugs can all push readings up.

## When Is a Cardiologist Needed?

### What if my pressure stays high on treatment?

Consider a patient who takes medicine every day, yet readings at the clinic and at home stay above target. This pattern is often called resistant hypertension. It generally means blood pressure remains above target despite three different classes of medicine, one of them a diuretic, at tolerated doses. Working out why takes time, and a cardiologist can be a useful partner in that search.

### Could something specific be causing it?

Sometimes a cause can be found. Narrowing of the kidney arteries, hormonal disorders, sleep apnea and some medicines or substances can all raise readings. Pressure that starts unusually young, rises suddenly or is hard to explain may warrant specialist investigation.

### Is the heart already involved?

Heart failure, coronary artery disease, an irregular rhythm such as atrial fibrillation, valve disease or an enlarged heart on tests all shift the picture. These conditions can influence which blood pressure medicine is most suitable, so specialist input often shapes the plan.

## What Should You Do Next?

- Keep a written log of readings, ideally taken at home. Bring your monitor to appointments so your technique and device can be checked.

- Take medicines as prescribed. If you have side effects or miss doses, tell your doctor. Stopping or changing a dose on your own can cause problems.

- Ask whether a specialist is needed, and what they would be checking.

- Find out whether the referral is for a one-time opinion or ongoing care. Many people keep their primary doctor and see a cardiologist only for specific questions.

## When Should You Seek Urgent Care?

:::emergency

Some symptoms should not wait for a routine appointment. Seek emergency care if [high blood pressure](/conditions/high-blood-pressure/) comes with chest pain, severe breathlessness, sudden weakness or numbness, difficulty speaking, a sudden severe headache, vision changes, confusion or fainting. These can signal serious complications.

:::

## Final Thoughts

A cardiology referral does not automatically mean the condition is severe. Staying with a primary care doctor does not mean you are getting less attention. What matters is how your readings respond to treatment and what other health conditions you have.
