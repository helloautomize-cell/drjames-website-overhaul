Blood sugar is not only a after-meal story. Your liver keeps adding glucose to the bloodstream between meals and through the night, which is how your brain gets fuel while you sleep. Insulin acts as the brake on that process. So when a fasting reading comes back high, the question is usually which part of this system is out of step: the liver, the insulin response, a hormone or medicine, or the test itself.

For the wider picture, see our guide on [prediabetes early warning signs](/health-library/diabetes-and-prediabetes-early-warning-signs-you-shouldnt-ignore/).

## How Does the Liver Keep Blood Sugar Steady?

Between meals, the liver breaks down stored glycogen and builds new glucose from other sources. Insulin tells it to slow down. In insulin resistance, the message gets weaker, so the liver goes on producing glucose even when insulin is circulating. This is one of the main reasons fasting levels creep upward in [prediabetes](/conditions/diabetes-and-prediabetes/) and type 2 diabetes.

### What Is the Dawn Phenomenon?

Cortisol and growth hormone rise in the early morning, nudging the liver to release extra glucose before you wake. Most people cover this with a bit more insulin. If the insulin response is less efficient, the rise becomes visible as a higher reading before breakfast. Clinicians call this the dawn phenomenon. A small morning increase is expected in many people. A large or repeated one deserves a conversation with your doctor.

## What Else Can Raise Blood Sugar Before Breakfast?

### Stress, Infection and Hormones

Adrenaline, cortisol and glucagon all push glucose upward. A fever, an infection, surgery, a painful night or intense emotional stress can produce a temporary rise that settles once the trigger passes. Overactive thyroid function and, less commonly, Cushing syndrome can keep levels elevated for longer.

### Medicines

Oral steroids such as prednisolone tend to raise glucose most in the afternoon and evening, though morning values can shift as well. Some antipsychotic medicines have a comparable effect. Diabetes medicines that lower sugar too far overnight can trigger a rebound high reading by morning. Insulin and other prescribed medicines should never be changed on your own. Timing and dose decisions belong with your prescriber.

### Sleep, Activity and Late Meals

A few nights of short or fragmented sleep can measurably reduce insulin sensitivity. Sleep apnea, where breathing repeatedly pauses during sleep, is closely tied to insulin resistance. Long stretches of sitting work in the same direction. A heavy or late dinner, or alcohol the previous evening, can keep glucose elevated into the next morning.

### Less Common Causes

Pancreatic disease can reduce insulin production. High blood sugar that appears alongside unexplained weight loss, particularly in an older adult, warrants a fuller investigation rather than a routine recheck.

## Could the Test Be Misleading?

It can be. Most laboratories require at least eight hours without food or caloric drinks. Sweetened tea or coffee, juice, milk and sugar-containing gum can all change the result. Blood left standing before analysis loses glucose to the cells it contains, which is why collection tubes include a preservative. Home meters are prone to error when strips have expired, the code does not match the meter, or the finger is not clean. A single high value is not a diagnosis, and a single normal value does not close the question.

## What Should You Do?

- Record the time of each reading, your last meal and drinks, your medicines, how you slept, and any recent illness.

- Ask for a fasting glucose test and an HbA1c, and confirm that the fasting instructions were followed correctly.

- If you test at home, log readings for one to two weeks so a pattern can emerge.

- Mention snoring, morning headaches or daytime sleepiness to your doctor, as these can point toward sleep apnea.

## When Should You See a Doctor?

Arrange a routine review if fasting readings are repeatedly 100 mg/dL or higher, especially with a family history, excess weight or high blood pressure.

:::emergency

Seek urgent care if high readings come with vomiting, drowsiness or confusion, deep or rapid breathing, a fruity odour on the breath, or intense thirst with heavy urination. Low readings accompanied by shaking, sweating or confusion also need prompt treatment. Follow the plan your doctor has already given you.

:::

## Final Thoughts

A high number without eating deserves attention, but it rarely means the worst. Most causes can be identified, and many improve once the underlying driver is addressed. Keep a clear record, test under proper conditions, and bring the full picture to your doctor.
