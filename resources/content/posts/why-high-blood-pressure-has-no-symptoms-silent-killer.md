Ask most people with [high blood pressure](/conditions/high-blood-pressure/) how they feel and you will hear some version of "fine." That answer is the core of the problem. High blood pressure is usually silent, and it does its damage quietly in the heart, brain, kidneys and eyes for years before anyone connects it to a symptom. That is where the name "silent killer" comes from.

For the wider picture, see our guide on [high blood pressure causes, risks and management](/health-library/understanding-high-blood-pressure-causes-risks-and-management/).

## Why Does High Blood Pressure Feel Like Nothing?

Blood pressure rarely jumps overnight. It creeps up over months and years, and the body makes room for it. Arteries thicken and stiffen, the heart adjusts to pushing against more resistance, and the new level gradually becomes the normal you live with every day. The body has no built-in alarm for this. A person can climb stairs, work a full shift and sleep well with readings that would concern a cardiologist.

## Why Is It Called the Silent Killer?

Sustained pressure wears on the inner lining of blood vessels, and that wear accumulates. Over time it can contribute to:

- Heart attack and heart failure

- Stroke

- Kidney damage, which can progress to kidney failure in some people

- Damage to the tiny vessels at the back of the eye

Many patients first learn they have hypertension after one of these events. The nickname is meant as a prompt to get measured, not as a forecast. Most people with raised pressure do not suffer a catastrophe, and treatment meaningfully reduces the risk when it is started early and maintained.

## Which Symptoms Mean You Need Urgent Help?

:::emergency

Severe elevations can produce symptoms, and a few of them are emergencies. Call emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department if high blood pressure comes with:

- A sudden, severe headache

- Chest pain or pressure

- Breathlessness

- Sudden weakness or numbness, often on one side of the body

- Slurred speech or a drooping face

- Confusion or new changes in vision

:::

A reading above 180/120 mmHg with any of these needs urgent assessment. Do not attribute these symptoms to blood pressure without a proper evaluation, since several serious conditions look similar at first.

## How Do You Find Out If You Have It?

Most healthy adults should have their pressure checked at least every few years. If you have diabetes, kidney disease, excess weight or a family history, ask for checks more often. A single reading rarely settles the question. Doctors generally want repeated readings on separate days before diagnosing hypertension.

If you use a home monitor, choose a validated upper-arm device rather than a wrist or finger model. Sit quietly for five minutes first, with your back supported and feet flat on the floor. Rest your arm on a table at heart level and take two readings about a minute apart. Keep a written log and bring it to your appointment. A log often changes the conversation, because it shows whether the high numbers appear only in the clinic or throughout the day.

## What Should You Do If a Reading Is High?

One high reading is a reason to repeat the measurement, not a reason to worry yourself into a state. If elevated readings keep coming back, arrange a review. Practical changes make a real difference for many people. These include eating less salt, especially the hidden salt in bread, sauces, instant meals and restaurant food, walking or exercising on most days, losing weight gradually if needed, cutting back on alcohol and stopping smoking.

If your doctor prescribes medicine, keep taking it as directed. Many people stop once their numbers improve, and the pressure can climb back up. Any change to a dose belongs with your doctor.

## When Should You See a Doctor?

[Book an appointment](/contact/) if you have never had your pressure measured. Book one if repeated readings are at or above 130/80 mmHg, a threshold many current guidelines use to define raised pressure. Book one if you have diabetes or kidney disease and have not had a recent check. Readings above 180/120 mmHg, or any of the symptoms listed above, need prompt medical attention.
