Get urgent care if the pain is sudden and severe, or if it comes with fever, vomiting blood, black stools, a hard swollen belly, fainting, or pain moving into the chest. Gastritis is inflammation of the stomach lining. It typically causes a burning or gnawing ache in the upper middle abdomen, often with nausea or feeling full after a few bites. H. pylori infection, regular use of anti-inflammatory painkillers and alcohol are the usual culprits, and it generally responds well to treatment once the cause is found.

[Abdominal pain](/conditions/digestive-problems/) can come from something as ordinary as trapped gas or [indigestion](/health-library/common-digestive-problems/), or from something that needs a surgeon, like appendicitis. Gastritis, constipation, stomach infections, gallstones and kidney stones fall somewhere in between. What usually tells them apart isn't the pain itself but the details around it: where it sits, how it started, and what else you've noticed.

Most stomach pain settles without any serious problem behind it. A small number of cases do need quick attention, though, and it helps to know which is which.

In this guide we go through the common causes of abdominal pain by location and pattern, take a closer look at gastritis (one of the most frequent reasons people come in with upper stomach pain), and list the warning signs you shouldn't sit on. It's written for adults in Muscat and elsewhere in Oman who want a clear starting point before they speak to a doctor.

## What Causes Abdominal Pain?

Nearly every organ in the abdomen can produce pain: the stomach, bowel, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, kidneys, bladder and appendix, and in women the uterus and ovaries. The muscles of the abdominal wall can hurt too. Occasionally the problem isn't in the abdomen at all. The heart and the lower parts of the lungs can both send pain there.

When a doctor sees someone with stomach pain, they're really asking three things. Where exactly does it hurt? How did it start, and how has it behaved since? And what else is going on, such as fever, vomiting, diarrhoea or changes when passing urine?

None of these answers settles the question alone. Put together with an examination, and sometimes a few tests, they usually do.

## What Can Abdominal Pain Mean by Location?

Doctors map the abdomen into regions because each one sits over a different set of organs. It's a useful guide, not a rule. Pain moves, spreads, and sometimes shows up somewhere unexpected.

### Upper middle abdomen (below the breastbone)

This is the area doctors call the epigastrium, and it's where a lot of everyday stomach pain lives. Gastritis, acid reflux and indigestion are the common causes. A peptic ulcer, which is a sore in the stomach or the first part of the small intestine, can hurt here as well.

Pancreatitis also causes pain in this spot, usually severe and often boring through to the back. Appendicitis sometimes begins here or near the navel before it settles lower down.

One cause is easy to miss. A heart attack can feel like indigestion or upper stomach discomfort, particularly in older adults, women and people with diabetes. If that discomfort comes with sweating, breathlessness, or pain moving into the chest, arm or jaw, treat it as an emergency.

### Upper right abdomen

Pain under the right ribs usually points toward the gallbladder or liver. Gallstones tend to cause attacks after a heavy or oily meal, and the pain can last anywhere from under an hour to several hours. When the gallbladder itself becomes inflamed, the pain is more constant and may bring a fever. Hepatitis and other liver problems can also cause an ache here.

### Upper left abdomen

Part of the stomach, the spleen and the tail of the pancreas sit on this side. Gastritis, indigestion and pancreatitis can all be felt here.

### Around the belly button

Stomach bugs, gas and small bowel problems often cause pain around the navel. It's also where appendicitis classically starts, before shifting to the lower right over several hours.

### Lower right abdomen

Appendicitis is the one people worry about, and rightly so. Kidney stones, hernias and, in women, ovarian cysts can also cause pain here. If lower right pain keeps getting worse over a few hours, especially with fever, nausea or no appetite, get it checked the same day.

### Lower left abdomen

Constipation is the most common reason for pain on this side. In older adults, diverticulitis (inflammation of small pouches in the bowel wall) is another important cause. Ovarian problems can cause left-sided pain in women too.

### Lower middle abdomen

Pain just above the pubic bone often comes from the bladder, for example a urinary infection, or from the uterus. Burning or frequent urination is a fairly strong hint that the urinary tract is involved.

### Side and back (flank)

Pain in the side or lower back, just under the ribs, is often kidney-related. A kidney stone is famous for pain that comes in intense waves and may travel toward the groin. A kidney infection tends to bring flank pain with fever and a general feeling of being unwell.

### Pain all over

Diffuse pain is common with gastroenteritis, constipation, IBS and gas. Less often, it's a sign of something serious like a bowel obstruction or peritonitis (inflammation of the lining of the abdomen). A belly that becomes hard, swollen and very tender is a red flag.

## What Can the Timing and Type of Pain Tell You?

How pain behaves is often as telling as where it is.

### Sudden or gradual?

Pain that arrives suddenly and severely is the kind that needs quick assessment. A perforated ulcer, a twisted or blocked bowel and a kidney stone can all start this way. Pain that builds steadily over hours is more typical of inflammation, as with appendicitis or gallbladder disease. Pain that has been coming and going for weeks or months is more often gastritis, reflux, IBS or constipation.

### Is it linked to food?

Pain soon after eating fits with gastritis, a stomach ulcer or plain indigestion. Pain after a large, rich meal raises the possibility of gallstones. With ulcers in the duodenum, some people find eating eases the pain for a while, only for it to come back a few hours later. Heartburn and a sour taste after meals, worse when you lie down, sound more like reflux.

### Cramping or constant?

Cramps that come in waves are common with gas, stomach bugs, constipation and kidney stones. A steady pain that doesn't let up is more in keeping with inflammation, such as appendicitis, pancreatitis or an inflamed gallbladder.

### Does it wake you at night?

Night pain can happen with ulcers and reflux. It can also be a clue that something needs investigating, so it's worth mentioning to your doctor.

## What Is Gastritis?

Gastritis means the lining of the stomach is inflamed. That lining normally has its own protective layer against stomach acid. When the protection is weakened, or the lining is irritated by something, inflammation follows.

It can be acute, coming on quickly after a clear trigger like a few days of painkillers or a heavy night of drinking. Or it can be chronic, developing quietly over months or years, often because of H. pylori.

Plenty of people with gastritis have obvious symptoms. Others have none at all and only find out when an endoscopy is done for a different reason.

## What Causes Gastritis?

### H. pylori infection

Helicobacter pylori is a bacterium that can live in the stomach lining for years. Many people carry it without any trouble. In others it causes long-standing gastritis and can lead to ulcers. It's one of the main causes of chronic gastritis worldwide, and clearing it gives the lining a chance to heal.

### Anti-inflammatory painkillers

Ibuprofen, diclofenac, naproxen and aspirin belong to a group called NSAIDs. Taken regularly, they can wear down the stomach's protective layer. The risk goes up with age, higher doses, longer use, and when these drugs are combined with steroids or blood thinners.

### Alcohol

Alcohol irritates the stomach lining directly, and heavy drinking is a well-known trigger.

### Less common causes

Major physical stress, such as serious surgery, severe burns or critical illness, can cause stress-related gastritis. In autoimmune gastritis, the body's immune system attacks the stomach lining, which can eventually lead to low vitamin B12. Bile flowing back from the small intestine can irritate the stomach too. Smoking doesn't usually cause gastritis on its own, but it slows healing and can make symptoms worse.

## What Are the Symptoms of Gastritis?

The classic complaint is a burning or gnawing ache in the upper middle abdomen. Alongside it, people often mention nausea, sometimes vomiting, bloating, belching, a poor appetite, or feeling full after only a few mouthfuls.

The trouble is that reflux, ulcers and gallbladder disease can produce almost the same list. That's why symptoms alone can't confirm gastritis.

### Can gastritis cause serious symptoms?

:::emergency

Sometimes. Gastritis or an ulcer can bleed. Vomiting blood or something that looks like coffee grounds, passing black and sticky stools, or suddenly feeling faint and weak are all signs of bleeding. Any of these needs urgent medical care.

:::

## Gastritis vs Acid Reflux: What Is the Difference?

People mix these up all the time, and it's understandable.

Gastritis is inflammation inside the stomach. It tends to cause pain or discomfort in the upper middle abdomen, with nausea or early fullness.

Acid reflux (GERD) is stomach acid coming back up into the food pipe. Its hallmark is heartburn, a burning feeling behind the breastbone, often with a sour or bitter taste in the mouth. It's usually worse after meals and when lying flat.

You can have both at once, and some treatments overlap. A doctor can help untangle which one is driving your symptoms.

## How Is Abdominal Pain Diagnosed?

It starts with talking. Expect your doctor to ask where the pain is, when it began and whether it has moved. They'll want to know what makes it better or worse and whether it's tied to eating, bowel movements or passing urine. Fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation and weight loss all matter.

They'll also ask about medicines, including anything bought over the counter and any supplements. Alcohol, smoking, and conditions like diabetes or heart disease are relevant, as is any past abdominal surgery. For women, questions about periods and the chance of pregnancy are routine.

Then comes the examination. Your doctor will press gently on different parts of your abdomen, looking for tenderness, swelling or signs of inflammation.

## What Tests May Be Needed?

Many people with abdominal pain don't need any tests. When they do, the choice depends on what the doctor suspects.

### Blood tests

These can show infection, anaemia, liver and kidney function, pancreatic enzymes and blood sugar. Unexplained anaemia can be a sign of slow bleeding from the stomach.

### Urine and stool tests

A urine test can pick up infection, blood from a kidney stone, or pregnancy. Stool tests can look for infection, hidden blood, or H. pylori.

### Testing for H. pylori

The usual options are a urea breath test, a stool antigen test, or a biopsy taken during endoscopy. Acid-reducing tablets and antibiotics can interfere with the results, so your doctor may give you instructions beforehand. Please don't stop any prescribed medicine on your own.

### Imaging

Ultrasound is often the first scan for the gallbladder, liver, kidneys and pelvic organs. A CT scan gives more detail and may be used when appendicitis, diverticulitis or a bowel obstruction is suspected.

### Upper endoscopy

In an upper endoscopy, or gastroscopy, a thin flexible camera goes through the mouth to look at the food pipe, stomach and duodenum. Small tissue samples can be taken at the same time. It's often recommended when symptoms don't settle with treatment, when there are warning signs, or when an older adult develops new indigestion that won't go away.

### ECG

If there's any chance upper abdominal pain is coming from the heart, especially in someone with diabetes, high blood pressure or a smoking history, an ECG is a quick and sensible check.

## How Do Doctors Tell Serious From Minor Causes?

There's no single test for this. Doctors weigh everything together.

Things that make them more concerned include pain that's sudden, severe or rapidly worsening, and pain that settles in one spot and keeps getting worse, particularly on the lower right. Fever, a rigid or very tender abdomen, repeated vomiting, blood in vomit or stool, dehydration, low blood pressure and yellowing of the eyes all raise the level of concern.

Who the patient is matters as well. Older adults, people with diabetes and people with weakened immunity can be quite unwell while having surprisingly mild symptoms.

On the other side, mild pain that comes and goes, is clearly linked to meals or bowel habits, and improves over a day or two is usually reassuring. Even then, pain that keeps coming back is worth getting looked at.

## How Is Gastritis Treated?

The aim is to calm the inflammation, let the lining heal, and deal with whatever caused it in the first place.

### Clearing H. pylori

If H. pylori turns up, treatment is usually a combination of antibiotics plus an acid-reducing medicine, taken for a fixed course. Finishing the whole course matters. A follow-up test afterwards is common, to make sure the infection has actually gone.

### Reducing stomach acid

Your doctor may use proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), which cut acid production and help the lining heal, or H2 blockers, which also lower acid. Antacids neutralise acid that's already there and give quick, short-lived relief.

These medicines are generally well tolerated. Long-term use should still be reviewed from time to time, and the right choice depends on your other conditions and medicines.

### Looking at your painkillers

If NSAIDs are part of the problem, your doctor may switch you to a different painkiller or add something to protect the stomach. If you take aspirin for your heart, don't stop it without talking to your doctor first. It may be doing an important job.

### Other causes

Autoimmune gastritis may need vitamin B12 checks and replacement. Bile reflux and stress-related gastritis are treated according to what's behind them.

## Can Gastritis Be Managed Without Medication?

Sometimes. Mild gastritis from an obvious trigger, like a short run of painkillers or a weekend of heavy drinking, often settles once that trigger is removed. H. pylori is different. It usually won't clear without proper treatment.

Lifestyle changes help with symptoms and support healing. They're not a replacement for getting checked if the problem keeps returning.

## What Lifestyle Changes Can Help With Gastritis and Stomach Pain?

### How you eat

Smaller meals at regular times tend to be easier on an irritated stomach than a couple of large ones. Eating slowly helps too. It's worth paying attention to what sets you off. Spicy, oily or fried food, very acidic foods, strong coffee and fizzy drinks bother some people and not others. If reflux is part of the picture, try not to lie down for two to three hours after eating.

### Fasting and festive meals

Some people find their stomach complains more during Ramadan, especially after a very large iftar or a rich late-night meal. Breaking the fast gently with water and something light before the main meal may help. If you have gastritis or an ulcer, or you take regular medicines, talk to your doctor before the fasting month begins.

### Alcohol and smoking

Cutting back on alcohol, or stopping, takes a direct irritant away from the stomach lining. Quitting smoking helps the lining heal and lowers ulcer risk.

### Painkillers

Try not to rely on anti-inflammatory painkillers without advice. A pharmacist or doctor can suggest something gentler for your situation.

### Stress

Everyday stress isn't the root cause of most gastritis, but it can make stomach symptoms feel worse. Sleep, regular activity and whatever genuinely helps you unwind are all worth protecting.

## What Complications Can Occur?

Most gastritis gets better with treatment. When it's left alone for a long time, it can lead to peptic ulcers or bleeding, either sudden or slow. Slow bleeding can cause iron deficiency anaemia without any obvious sign. Autoimmune gastritis can lead to low vitamin B12.

Over many years, long-standing inflammation, particularly from untreated H. pylori, can change the stomach lining in ways that slightly raise the risk of stomach cancer in some people. That's the main reason persistent symptoms should be properly assessed rather than managed with antacids indefinitely.

## When Is Abdominal Pain Serious?

### Go to emergency care straight away if you have:

:::emergency

- Sudden, severe abdominal pain

- Pain with chest discomfort, breathlessness or sweating, or pain spreading to the arm, neck or jaw

- Vomiting blood, or vomit that looks like coffee grounds

- Black, tarry stools, or a lot of blood in the stool

- A hard, swollen abdomen that's very painful to touch

- Fainting, confusion or severe dizziness

- Vomiting and bloating with no stool or gas passing

- Severe pain in pregnancy, or abdominal pain when pregnancy is possible

- High fever with abdominal pain

In Oman, go to the nearest emergency department.

:::

### Book a doctor's appointment soon if:

- The pain has lasted more than a few days, or keeps coming back

- It wakes you at night

- You're losing weight without trying

- You have trouble swallowing

- Nausea or vomiting won't settle

- Your skin or eyes look yellow

- Your bowel habits have changed for more than a few weeks

- You're an older adult with new indigestion that isn't going away

- There's stomach or bowel cancer in your family

- Symptoms return after you've finished treatment

## Common Myths About Abdominal Pain and Gastritis

### Myth: Spicy food causes stomach ulcers.

Fact: Most ulcers come from H. pylori or regular anti-inflammatory painkillers. Spicy food doesn't cause them, though it can aggravate symptoms if you already have gastritis or reflux.

### Myth: Stress is the main cause of gastritis.

Fact: Everyday stress can make symptoms feel worse, but it's rarely the underlying cause. Severe physical stress from serious illness is a recognised cause. Most chronic gastritis, though, is down to H. pylori, medicines or alcohol.

### Myth: A glass of milk heals gastritis.

Fact: Milk might soothe things for a short while, but it can also prompt the stomach to make more acid afterwards. It isn't a treatment.

### Myth: If the pain went away, it was nothing.

Fact: Gallstones and ulcers often cause pain that comes and goes. Recurring pain is worth checking, even when each episode settles.

### Myth: Antacids are fine to take every day indefinitely.

Fact: The occasional antacid is usually harmless. Needing them most days for weeks is a sign the cause should be looked into.

## Final Thoughts

Most abdominal pain has a cause that can be managed, and gastritis usually improves well once its trigger is identified. The hard part is that very different conditions can feel alike, and a few of them can't wait. Noticing where the pain is, how it behaves and what comes with it will help you decide whether to watch and wait, [book an appointment](/contact/), or get help now.

If you've been living with ongoing stomach pain, indigestion or suspected gastritis in Muscat, seeing an Internal Medicine Specialist can help pin down the cause and arrange the right tests. It also means your treatment is planned around your wider health, including conditions like diabetes, high blood pressure or heart disease, which can change how abdominal problems show up.
