Most people do not start by looking for a specialist. They start with a number on a lab report, a glucose reading that seemed off, or a parent who has just been diagnosed and now has a long list of questions. Then comes the practical problem: who should you actually see? For most adults with [diabetes](/conditions/diabetes-and-prediabetes/), an internist is a sound place to begin. An endocrinologist is a physician with additional training in hormones and metabolism, and there are clear situations where that training makes a real difference. Knowing which situation you are in can save you weeks of waiting.

For the wider picture, see our guide on [early warning signs of diabetes and prediabetes](/health-library/diabetes-and-prediabetes-early-warning-signs-you-shouldnt-ignore/).

## What Does an Internist Do for Diabetes?

An internist looks at the whole adult, which suits diabetes well, since it rarely travels alone. At a typical review, your blood sugar and HbA1c trends are checked alongside blood pressure, cholesterol and kidney function. Early signs of nerve or eye involvement are screened for, and common diabetes medicines can be started and adjusted. When something falls outside routine care, the internist refers you on to eye, foot, kidney or hormone specialists.

## When Is an Endocrinologist the Better Choice?

Endocrinologists manage hormone problems every day, and diabetes is one of the largest. A referral tends to make sense in a few situations:

- Type 1 diabetes, whether suspected or already confirmed. Insulin is needed from the start, and doses usually need close, frequent review.

- Blood sugar that stays high despite treatment, or that repeatedly drops too low.

- An insulin pump or continuous glucose monitor, where the data needs expert interpretation.

- A thyroid or adrenal problem that may be pushing blood sugar around.

- Kidney or nerve complications that are progressing.

- Pregnancy with diabetes, or planning one, which needs close coordination between teams.

## Does a Referral Mean Something Is Seriously Wrong?

A referral letter can feel alarming, especially when the word "specialist" appears on it. In most cases, it reflects a need for closer attention rather than a sign that treatment has failed. Asking your doctor what they hope the specialist will answer usually makes the visit far more useful.

## How Should You Prepare for the Right Appointment?

The first visit goes better with a little preparation. Bring your recent HbA1c and blood sugar readings, the full list of medicines and supplements you take, and any kidney, eye or foot test results. If you log readings on a meter or app, bring that too. It also helps to know whether your diabetes type has been confirmed, because type 1 and type 2 follow quite different treatment paths.

## When Should You Seek Urgent Care?

:::emergency

Some symptoms should not wait for any appointment. Seek urgent care for persistent vomiting that prevents you from keeping fluids down, fast or deep breathing, drowsiness or confusion, or fainting. A low blood sugar that does not come up after treatment also needs immediate help. These can be signs of emergencies such as diabetic ketoacidosis or severe hypoglycemia.

:::

## Final Thoughts

Either specialist can provide good care. The deciding factors are the type of diabetes you have, how it responds to treatment, and whether complications or other hormone problems are involved. Diabetes is managed over years, so continuity matters. A doctor who knows your history and follows up reliably often serves you better than any title does.
