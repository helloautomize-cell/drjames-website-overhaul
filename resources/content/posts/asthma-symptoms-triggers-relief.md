Doctors diagnose [asthma](/conditions/asthma-and-breathing/) from your history, an examination and breathing tests such as spirometry. Most people are treated with an inhaler containing an anti-inflammatory corticosteroid, along with a plan for quick relief. If you can't finish a sentence, your lips look blue, or your reliever isn't working, get emergency care straight away.

Asthma is a long-term condition in which the airways in the lungs stay inflamed and overly sensitive, so they tighten up when they meet certain triggers. That tightening shows up as wheezing, coughing, a tight chest or shortness of breath, and the symptoms usually come and go. Get the diagnosis right, use the right inhaler properly, and learn what sets your chest off, and most adults can keep asthma well under control.

People tend to start searching for answers at a particular moment. Maybe a cold has left behind a cough that simply won't settle. Maybe they woke at 3 a.m. feeling as though they couldn't get the air out. Or they've had asthma for years and quietly suspect they reach for their blue inhaler far more often than they should.

Whichever of those sounds familiar, this guide covers what asthma actually is, the triggers that matter most (including a few that are especially relevant in Oman), how doctors confirm it, how it's treated, and the signs that mean you shouldn't wait.

## What Is Asthma?

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory condition of the airways, the branching tubes that carry air in and out of your lungs. In someone with asthma, the lining of those tubes is irritated much of the time, even on good days.

When a trigger comes along, the airways react in three ways at once. The band of muscle wrapped around each airway squeezes (doctors call this bronchoconstriction). The lining swells. And the airway starts producing thick mucus that clogs the smaller passages.

Narrower tubes mean air has a harder time escaping. That's where the whistling wheeze comes from, and why people with asthma often describe the odd feeling of being unable to breathe out properly rather than in.

Two things set asthma apart from many other lung problems. The narrowing varies: you might be fine for weeks, then struggle for a few days. And it's usually reversible, easing on its own or with treatment. Both features help doctors separate asthma from conditions like COPD.

### Can you get asthma for the first time as an adult?

You can. Asthma often begins in childhood, but plenty of people are diagnosed in their thirties, forties or later. Sometimes it follows a bad chest infection. Sometimes it's linked to something breathed in at work. Often there's no obvious reason at all. People who had childhood asthma can also find it returns years after they thought they'd outgrown it.

## What Are the Symptoms of Asthma?

Four symptoms come up again and again:

- Wheezing, a whistling or squeaky sound, mostly when you breathe out

- Shortness of breath, sometimes with activities that never used to bother you

- Chest tightness, which people describe as pressure, squeezing, or a band around the chest

- Coughing, usually dry, and often worse at night, first thing in the morning, after exercise or around a trigger

You don't need all four to have asthma. Some people just cough. Others only notice it when they climb stairs or play football, and feel perfectly normal at rest.

### What are the early signs that asthma is getting worse?

Asthma rarely goes from calm to severe without dropping hints first. The clues are often small. You might find yourself using your reliever more than usual, waking at night with a cough, or getting breathless doing things you normally manage without thinking. A cold that "settles on your chest" and hangs around is another. If you check your peak flow at home, readings below your usual best tell the same story.

Catching these early gives you time to follow your action plan or speak to your doctor before a proper attack builds.

### Why is asthma often worse at night?

A few things work against you at night. Airway function naturally dips in the early hours. You're lying close to dust mites in your pillow and mattress for hours at a stretch. Lying flat can make acid reflux worse in some people. And an air conditioner running all night can leave the air cool and dry.

Whatever the cause, waking up with asthma symptoms is a sign that it isn't fully controlled. It's worth raising with your doctor rather than just putting up with it.

### Can asthma cause just a cough?

Yes. In cough-variant asthma, a dry, nagging cough may be the only symptom. There's no obvious wheeze, so it's easy to blame a lingering infection. Clues include a cough that's worse at night, after exercise, in cold air, or even after a good laugh. If a cough has dragged on for weeks, it deserves a proper look.

## What Triggers Asthma?

A trigger is anything that irritates sensitive airways and sets off symptoms. Most people have more than one, and they aren't the same for everyone. Working out your own is one of the most useful things you can do.

### Colds, flu and other infections

Viral infections are among the most common reasons adults with asthma have a flare-up. They add to the inflammation that's already there, and the cough and tightness can linger for weeks after the fever has gone.

### Allergens

In allergic asthma, the immune system overreacts to things that are harmless to most people. House dust mites are a big one, living in bedding, mattresses, carpets and sofas. Pet dander from cats and dogs, mould spores in damp bathrooms or neglected AC units, pollen, and cockroach droppings can all play a part.

### Dust and sand

Anyone who has lived through a dusty, windy spell in Muscat knows how thick the air can get. For some people with asthma, airborne dust and sand are a major trigger. Construction dust and traffic fumes add to the problem in busier areas.

### Smoke, incense and strong smells

Tobacco smoke is one of the worst irritants, and that includes second-hand smoke and shisha. Something many families don't think about is incense. Bakhoor and oud are part of daily life in many homes, and their smoke can set off coughing and tightness in sensitive people. Strong perfumes, cleaning sprays and paint fumes can do the same.

### Heat, cold and the air conditioner

It isn't only cold weather that causes trouble. Stepping repeatedly from 40-degree heat into a heavily air-conditioned room can irritate the airways. High humidity, cold dry air and thunderstorms affect some people too.

### Exercise

Exercise can narrow the airways during or just after activity, particularly in cold or dry air. This is called exercise-induced bronchoconstriction. It isn't a reason to stop being active. The goal is to control asthma well enough that you can exercise comfortably.

### Certain medicines

A subset of people with asthma react to aspirin or other anti-inflammatory painkillers such as ibuprofen. Beta-blockers, used for some heart and blood pressure conditions and found in some eye drops, can also make asthma worse in certain people. Tell every doctor and pharmacist you see that you have asthma. And please don't stop a prescribed medicine on your own because of this; discuss it first.

### Things you breathe in at work

Occupational asthma is caused or worsened by workplace exposures such as flour dust, wood dust, cleaning chemicals, paints, glues or animal proteins. The giveaway is often that symptoms ease on weekends or holidays and return once you're back at work.

### Stress and strong emotions

Anxiety, stress, crying and even hearty laughter change the way you breathe, and that can be enough to tip sensitive airways into symptoms.

### Acid reflux

When stomach acid creeps up into the food pipe, it can aggravate asthma, especially at night.

## Who Is More Likely to Get Asthma?

There's rarely a single cause. Asthma usually comes from a mix of inherited tendency and what you're exposed to over your life.

Your risk is higher if a parent or sibling has asthma, or if you have allergic conditions such as eczema, hay fever or food allergies. Smoking, long-term exposure to other people's smoke, obesity, regular contact with irritants at work, and a history of serious chest infections all add to it.

None of these guarantees you'll develop asthma. And some people with asthma have none of them.

## Is It Asthma or Something Else?

Wheezing and breathlessness aren't unique to asthma, which is exactly why guessing is risky.

COPD is the condition most often confused with it. COPD is usually linked to years of smoking or smoke exposure, tends to appear later in life, and causes narrowing that doesn't bounce back the way asthma does. Chest infections such as bronchitis or pneumonia can cause similar symptoms in the short term.

Some causes surprise people. Heart failure can produce breathlessness and even a wheeze, sometimes called "cardiac asthma." Vocal cord dysfunction, where the vocal cords close when they should open, can look very much like asthma. Acid reflux and post-nasal drip from allergic rhinitis both cause chronic coughs. Anaemia, thyroid problems and anxiety-related breathing patterns can all leave you feeling short of breath.

It's also possible to have more than one condition, asthma and COPD together, for instance. This is where an internal medicine specialist is useful: the heart, lungs, weight, reflux and blood tests all get looked at together rather than in isolation.

## How Is Asthma Diagnosed?

No single test confirms asthma in everyone. Your doctor puts together your story, an examination and breathing tests.

### What will the doctor ask you?

Expect questions about what your symptoms are and how often they happen, whether they're worse at night or in the morning, and whether exercise, dust, pets or smoke bring them on. Your doctor will probably ask whether things improve on days off, whether you or your family have allergies or eczema, whether you smoke, which medicines you take, and whether you've ever needed emergency treatment for your breathing.

A simple tip: jot down your symptoms and possible triggers for a week or two before the appointment. It makes the conversation far more productive.

### Which tests might you need?

Spirometry is the most common test. You take a deep breath and blow out as hard and fast as you can into a machine that measures how much air comes out and how quickly. It's often repeated after a puff of reliever. A clear improvement supports asthma.

Peak flow monitoring uses a small handheld meter you blow into at home, usually morning and evening for a couple of weeks. The pattern of ups and downs can reveal the variability typical of asthma.

A FeNO test measures nitric oxide in your breath, a marker of a particular type of airway inflammation. You just breathe steadily into a device. It takes a minute or two.

A bronchial challenge test may be used when the picture is unclear. You inhale a substance that causes mild narrowing in people with asthma, under close supervision.

Allergy tests, either skin prick or blood tests, can pin down triggers like dust mites or cat dander.

A chest X-ray doesn't diagnose asthma, but it can rule out other problems. Blood tests, including a blood eosinophil count, sometimes help guide treatment choices.

One thing to know: normal results on a good day don't always exclude asthma, because it fluctuates. Your doctor may repeat tests or ask you to monitor at home.

## How Is Asthma Treated?

There's no cure yet, but asthma can usually be controlled very well. The aim is simple enough: few or no symptoms, normal sleep, a normal working and active life, no attacks, and healthy lungs in the long run.

Treatment is tailored to you and adjusted over time. What works for your brother or colleague might not be right for you.

### Preventer inhalers

Inhaled corticosteroids are the backbone of treatment for most people. They calm the inflammation in the airways and cut the risk of attacks. They don't work instantly; they build up protection over time, which is why they're taken regularly or as directed, even when you feel fine.

Because the medicine goes straight to the lungs, very little reaches the rest of the body compared with steroid tablets. The side effects people notice most are a hoarse voice and oral thrush. Rinsing your mouth and gargling after each use, and using a spacer if advised, helps prevent both.

### Reliever inhalers

Relievers open narrowed airways quickly. For decades, many people were given only a short-acting blue reliever such as salbutamol and nothing else. That approach has changed. Current international asthma guidance no longer recommends treating adults and teenagers with a short-acting reliever alone, because it eases symptoms without treating the inflammation underneath.

Instead, many adults are now prescribed a combination inhaler containing an inhaled corticosteroid plus formoterol, a bronchodilator that acts quickly. Depending on your situation, it may be used only when needed, or daily and when needed. Your doctor will explain which fits you.

Here's the part worth underlining: if you need your reliever often, your asthma isn't well controlled. That's a reason to see your doctor, not a reason to keep puffing.

### Extra treatments when one isn't enough

If symptoms continue, your doctor may add a long-acting bronchodilator (often in the same inhaler as the steroid), a different type called a long-acting muscarinic antagonist, or a tablet called a leukotriene receptor antagonist, which helps some people, particularly those with allergies.

### Severe asthma

A small number of people still struggle despite good technique, taking treatment faithfully and higher-step medicines. They may be referred for specialist assessment. Some are candidates for biologic medicines, injections that target specific parts of the inflammation. These are chosen after detailed testing and given under specialist care.

### Steroid tablets

A short course of steroid tablets is often used to settle an asthma attack, and it works well. The catch is that repeated courses carry side effects over time. If you're needing them more than occasionally, your long-term plan needs rethinking.

Never start, stop or change an asthma medicine without speaking to your doctor. Stopping a preventer because you "feel better now" is one of the most common reasons asthma flares up again.

## Why Does Inhaler Technique Matter So Much?

An inhaler only helps if the medicine actually reaches your lungs. A surprising number of people use theirs incorrectly for years without knowing, so much of each dose ends up on the back of the throat.

The usual mistakes are small. Not breathing out fully first. Pressing the canister too early or too late. Breathing in too hard for one type of device or too gently for another. Forgetting to hold your breath afterwards. Using an inhaler that's quietly run out or expired.

Different devices need different techniques. A spacer, a plastic chamber that clips onto a standard puffer, makes it much easier to get the dose where it needs to go. Ask your doctor, nurse or pharmacist to watch you use your inhaler, and have it checked again at every review.

## Can Asthma Be Controlled Without Medication?

For most people, no, not safely. The airway inflammation is still there between flare-ups, and anti-inflammatory treatment is what lowers the risk of a serious attack.

A few people with very mild, infrequent symptoms may only need treatment as required, but that's a decision to make with a doctor after proper testing.

What lifestyle measures can do is reduce how often you need your medicine and how bad flare-ups get. The best results usually come from combining the two. Inhalers without attention to triggers, or trigger avoidance without the right medicine, both tend to leave people with more symptoms than they need to have.

## What Everyday Changes Help Asthma?

Get away from smoke. Quitting smoking is one of the most valuable things a person with asthma can do. Avoid second-hand smoke and shisha too. If incense clearly sets you off, consider burning it less often, in a well-ventilated room, or away from where you sleep. If you smoke and want to stop, your doctor can talk you through the options.

Tackle dust mites. Wash sheets and pillowcases regularly in hot water. Allergen-proof covers for mattresses and pillows help some people. Less clutter, fewer heavy curtains and fewer soft furnishings in the bedroom all reduce dust. If pets are a trigger, keep them out of the bedroom.

Look after the air conditioner. Clean or change the filters regularly and have units serviced so dust and mould don't build up. Try not to set the temperature very low if sudden temperature swings bother your chest.

Plan around dusty days. When the air is heavy with dust, keep windows shut, cut down time outside, save outdoor exercise for another day, and keep your reliever with you.

Keep moving. Regular exercise does your lungs and heart good. If it triggers symptoms, talk to your doctor about adjusting your treatment or using your inhaler before activity. A gradual warm-up helps too.

Watch your weight. Carrying extra weight can make asthma harder to control, and gradual weight loss often makes breathing easier.

Stay up to date with vaccines. Since infections are such a common trigger, ask your doctor whether the yearly flu vaccine and others, such as pneumococcal or COVID-19 vaccines, are right for you.

Treat the conditions that travel with asthma. Allergic rhinitis, acid reflux and sleep apnea can all make asthma worse. Getting them under control often helps your breathing too.

## What Is an Asthma Action Plan?

An asthma action plan is a short written plan you agree with your doctor. It spells out which medicines you take every day, how to spot asthma getting worse, what to do when it does, when to call your doctor, and when to go straight to emergency.

Many plans use symptoms or peak flow readings, often split into colour-coded zones, so you don't have to guess in the moment. Keep a copy at home, a photo on your phone, and make sure family members know where it is.

## What Complications Can Asthma Cause?

Well-controlled asthma causes few long-term problems for most people. Poorly controlled asthma is a different story.

:::emergency

Attacks can be severe and, occasionally, life-threatening. Frequent flare-ups mean emergency visits, hospital stays, missed work and broken sleep. Repeated steroid tablet courses bring their own side effects. In some people with long-standing uncontrolled inflammation, the airways gradually become more permanently narrowed, a process known as airway remodelling. Living with unpredictable breathing also takes an emotional toll, and anxiety and low mood are more common in people whose asthma isn't controlled.

:::

The encouraging part is that regular reviews and consistent treatment cut these risks considerably.

## What Does an Asthma Attack Feel Like?

During an attack, symptoms build quickly and don't settle the way they normally would. Breathlessness keeps getting worse. The wheeze, cough or tightness becomes severe. Talking, eating or sleeping gets difficult. You breathe faster than usual, and your reliever either barely helps or wears off within a short time.

Follow your action plan if you have one. If things aren't improving, get medical help promptly.

## When Should You See a Doctor About Asthma or Breathing Problems?

### Get emergency help now if:

:::emergency

- You're too breathless to speak in full sentences, eat or walk

- Your lips, tongue or fingertips look blue or grey

- Your reliever isn't helping, or you need it again very soon after using it

- You feel drowsy, confused, exhausted or faint

- The muscles in your neck or between your ribs pull in with each breath

- Your breathing is very fast, or the wheeze goes quiet while you're still struggling, which can mean the airways are severely narrowed

- You have chest pain along with breathlessness

In Oman, go to the nearest emergency department. Don't wait to see whether it passes.

:::

### Book an appointment if:

You have wheezing, a lingering cough or breathlessness that nobody has looked into. Or you're using your reliever more than advised, waking at night with symptoms, or finding that asthma limits your work, exercise or daily routine. The same goes if you've needed steroid tablets or an emergency visit, still feel short of breath despite your inhalers, aren't sure your technique is right, are pregnant or planning a pregnancy, or you're over 40 with new symptoms, particularly if you've smoked.

For adults in Muscat, an internal medicine specialist can assess breathing symptoms alongside the rest of your health, including your heart, weight, allergies, reflux and diabetes, and arrange further tests or a specialist referral if needed.

## Common Myths About Asthma

Myth: "Asthma is a children's condition." Asthma can start at any age. Adult-onset asthma is common and often missed, because symptoms get blamed on infections, getting older or being unfit.

Myth: "I feel fine, so I can stop my preventer." Feeling fine is often the preventer doing its job. Inflammation can carry on quietly without symptoms. If you'd like to reduce your treatment, talk to your doctor about stepping down safely.

Myth: "Steroid inhalers are like the steroids bodybuilders use." They're completely different. Asthma inhalers contain corticosteroids, which reduce inflammation. They aren't anabolic steroids, and the dose reaching your body is small. Doctors aim for the lowest dose that keeps you well.

Myth: "People with asthma shouldn't exercise." Being active is encouraged. With good control and a sensible plan, most people with asthma can exercise comfortably, and plenty of elite athletes have asthma.

Myth: "My blue inhaler works, so using it a lot is fine." Needing it often means your asthma isn't controlled. That calls for a treatment review, not more puffs.

Myth: "No wheeze means no asthma." Some people only ever cough, or feel tight or breathless, without any wheeze.
