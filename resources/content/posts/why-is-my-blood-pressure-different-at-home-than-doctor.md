A common scenario goes like this. The nurse wraps the cuff, the screen shows a number, and it is noticeably higher than the reading you took on your kitchen monitor that morning. So which one is correct? Usually, both are. The gap is one of the most common things patients ask about, and it tends to reveal something useful rather than a faulty machine.

For the wider picture, see our guide on [high blood pressure causes, risks and management](/health-library/understanding-high-blood-pressure-causes-risks-and-management/).

## What Is White-Coat Hypertension?

Many people tense up in a clinic. The waiting room, the unfamiliar equipment, and the knowledge that a number is about to be recorded can all push pressure up for a few minutes. Doctors are well aware of this, which is why the first reading of a visit is often set aside and a second one is taken after a short rest.

This is called white-coat hypertension. It is a genuine physical response and does not mean your concerns are exaggerated. It does mean that one clinic reading may overstate your usual level.

## What Is Masked Hypertension?

The reverse pattern is less obvious and, in some ways, more worrying. The clinic number looks reassuring, but pressure runs higher during ordinary life: on a demanding workday, during a stressful week, or overnight. Nothing feels wrong, so there is no obvious reason to suspect a problem. Masked hypertension is worth looking for in people with diabetes, obesity, or kidney disease, and in people who smoke.

## Why Does My Reading Change?

A brisk walk to the car, a strong coffee, a cigarette, or a full bladder can each shift the number. Technique matters just as much. A cuff that is too small, an arm resting below heart level, or a conversation during the measurement can all distort a result. Most of these are easy to fix, and they are worth checking before you draw any conclusions.

## How Should You Measure Blood Pressure at Home?

Avoid exercise, smoking, and caffeine for about 30 minutes beforehand. Empty your bladder, then sit quietly for five minutes with your back supported and both feet flat on the floor. Rest your bare upper arm on a table so the cuff sits at heart level. Stay silent while the monitor runs, and take a second reading about a minute later.

Upper-arm devices with a properly fitted cuff are generally more dependable than wrist models. Take your monitor to an appointment at least once, so your doctor can compare it with the clinic equipment.

## How Long Should I Monitor?

Most doctors want to see a pattern rather than a single snapshot. That usually means several days of readings, often morning and evening. Some patients are asked to wear a device that records pressure at regular intervals across 24 hours, including during sleep. This is called ambulatory monitoring, and it can show what happens during normal daily life in a way home readings sometimes cannot.

## Which Reading Counts?

Your doctor will not simply pick the lower number or the higher one. They look at the whole record, your overall risk profile, and whether each reading was taken properly. A neat log with dates and times often answers more questions than a pile of scattered numbers.

## When Should You See a Doctor?

:::emergency

Book a review if your home readings stay high, if they regularly differ from clinic numbers, or if pressure stays high while you are on treatment. Seek urgent care if a very high reading comes with chest pain, severe breathlessness, sudden weakness or numbness, slurred speech, confusion, or changes in vision.

:::
