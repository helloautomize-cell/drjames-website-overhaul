## Are Medications Now Available for Sleep Apnea?

For the wider picture, see our guide on [Common Sleep Disorders: Symptoms, Causes & Health Effects](/health-library/common-sleep-disorders-symptoms-health-effects/).

For years, treatment for obstructive [sleep apnea](/conditions/sleep-apnea/) (OSA) has focused on approaches such as positive airway pressure therapy, oral appliances, weight management, and selected surgical procedures.

That picture has changed.

In December 2024, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved tirzepatide as the first medication specifically indicated for moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity. The approval is for use alongside a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity.

This is an important development, but it does not mean that medication has replaced CPAP or that everyone with sleep apnea should take a drug.

## How Does Tirzepatide Help Sleep Apnea?

Tirzepatide is a medication that acts on GIP and GLP-1 hormone receptors. It reduces appetite and food intake and can produce significant weight loss.

In people with obesity, excess tissue around the upper airway can contribute to airway narrowing during sleep. Reducing body weight may therefore improve obstructive sleep apnea.

In the clinical studies supporting its OSA approval, adults with obesity and moderate-to-severe OSA experienced significant reductions in the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) compared with placebo after 52 weeks. AHI is a measurement of how often breathing stops or becomes significantly reduced during sleep.

## It Is Not a Universal Sleep Apnea Medication

The FDA indication is specifically for adults with obesity and moderate-to-severe OSA.

That means someone with mild sleep apnea, someone without obesity, or someone with a different type of sleep-related breathing disorder should not assume that tirzepatide is appropriate.

A doctor needs to assess the individual's diagnosis, medical history, weight-related health conditions, current medications, and treatment goals.

## Does Medication Replace CPAP?

No.

Positive airway pressure (PAP), including CPAP, remains an important established treatment for obstructive sleep apnea.

In the trials supporting tirzepatide's OSA indication, one study included people using PAP therapy, while another included people who were unable or unwilling to use it.

For some patients, medication may become part of a broader treatment plan rather than a simple replacement for existing therapy.

## Other Treatment Options Still Matter

Depending on the individual, treatment may include:

- CPAP or another form of PAP therapy

- A custom oral appliance

- Weight-management strategies

- Positional therapy

- Treatment of nasal or upper-airway problems

- Selected surgical procedures

The right approach depends on the severity and type of sleep apnea, anatomy, symptoms, medical conditions, and ability to tolerate different treatments.

## What Should Patients Know About Side Effects?

Tirzepatide can cause side effects, including nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, constipation, abdominal discomfort, fatigue, and reflux.

The FDA also lists important warnings and precautions, including risks involving the gallbladder, pancreas, kidney function, allergic reactions, and other conditions. It should not be used by people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or with Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2.

This is why tirzepatide should be prescribed and monitored by an appropriate healthcare professional.

## What Is the Next Step If You Have Sleep Apnea?

Medication should not be considered before confirming the diagnosis and understanding its severity.

If you have loud snoring, witnessed breathing pauses, nighttime gasping, morning headaches, or significant daytime sleepiness, speak with a healthcare professional. A sleep study may be recommended to determine whether sleep apnea is present and how severe it is.

Once the diagnosis is established, your doctor can discuss whether PAP therapy, an oral appliance, weight management, medication, or another treatment is appropriate.

## When to See a Doctor

:::emergency

Talk to your doctor before starting or stopping any sleep apnea treatment. Seek urgent care for chest pain, severe shortness of breath, persistent vomiting or severe abdominal pain while on injectable medicines.

:::

## Final Thoughts

New medication options are expanding the way doctors can approach obstructive sleep apnea, particularly for adults with obesity. Tirzepatide represents an important development, but it is not a universal replacement for CPAP or other established treatments. If you have symptoms of sleep apnea, the first step is an appropriate evaluation and diagnosis. From there, your doctor can help determine whether medication, PAP therapy, an oral appliance, weight management, or a combination of approaches makes the most sense for you.
