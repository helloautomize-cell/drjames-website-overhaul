Anxiety and an overactive [thyroid](/conditions/thyroid-disorders/) can feel surprisingly similar. Both can cause a racing heart, restlessness, poor sleep and irritability. Many people wonder whether their anxiety is really a thyroid problem. Sometimes it is, but often it is not, and a simple blood test can help clarify.

For the wider picture, see our guide on [Thyroid Disorders Explained: Hypothyroidism vs. Hyperthyroidism](/health-library/hypothyroidism-vs-hyperthyroidism-symptoms/).

## Why Can an Overactive Thyroid Feel Like Anxiety?

Excess thyroid hormone speeds up many body processes, increasing heart rate and nervous system activity. This produces physical symptoms that the brain interprets as anxiety.

## What Symptoms Overlap?

- Nervousness or feeling on edge

- Palpitations or a racing heart

- Trouble sleeping

- Irritability

- Difficulty concentrating

- Sweating

## Which Symptoms Point More Toward the Thyroid?

- Unexplained weight loss despite good appetite

- Heat intolerance and excessive sweating

- Fine tremor in the hands

- Frequent bowel movements

- Fast heart rate even at rest

- Eye changes such as bulging or grittiness in Graves' disease

- A swelling at the front of the neck

## What About Hypothyroidism?

An underactive thyroid more often causes fatigue, low mood, slowed thinking and feeling cold. Mood changes can occur, but anxiety-like racing symptoms are less typical.

## What Are Common Myths?

### Myth: all unexplained anxiety is a thyroid problem

Anxiety has many causes, including stress, life events, caffeine, medicines and mental health conditions.

### Myth: a normal thyroid test means nothing is wrong

It means the thyroid is not the cause, but anxiety itself is real and treatable.

### Myth: treating the thyroid will always fix anxiety

When hyperthyroidism is the cause, treatment often improves anxiety, but some people need additional support.

## When Should You Get Tested?

Consider a TSH test if anxiety comes with weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor, a fast resting heart rate, neck swelling, eye changes, a family history of thyroid disease or a recent pregnancy. Your doctor will decide.

## How Are They Treated?

If hyperthyroidism is confirmed, treatment may include antithyroid medicines, beta blockers to ease symptoms, radioactive iodine or surgery. If anxiety is the cause, options include psychological therapy, lifestyle changes and sometimes medicines.

## When Should You Seek Urgent Care?

:::emergency

Seek urgent care for chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness, a very fast or irregular heartbeat or thoughts of self-harm.

:::
