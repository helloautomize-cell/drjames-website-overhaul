## Who should see a physician about this

- Repeated sneezing, itchy eyes or a blocked nose that comes in a pattern.
- Hives, itching or rashes that keep returning.
- Suspected food, medicine or insect allergy.
- A chronic cough or wheeze that may be allergy-driven.
- You had a severe reaction before and want a clear plan.

## What happens at your visit

The history does most of the work: what appears, when, how fast, and what makes it better or worse. Dr. James examines your nose, throat, skin and chest, and looks for patterns like seasonal flares, home versus work differences, and links to foods or medicines.

Testing is suggested only when it changes management: allergy blood tests or a referral for skin testing when the trigger stays unclear, and an action plan if you have had severe reactions.

## How to prepare

- Keep a short diary of symptoms: what you ate, where you were, what touched your skin.
- Bring photos of rashes or swelling taken when they appeared.
- Bring all medicines and supplements, including eye drops and nasal sprays.

## After your visit

You leave with the most likely trigger identified, an avoidance plan that is actually doable, and symptom treatment suited to you. If a severe allergy is suspected, an emergency action plan is written out clearly for you and your family.

## When to get help straight away

Anaphylaxis is a medical emergency: swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, difficulty breathing or swallowing, widespread hives with dizziness, vomiting or collapse. Go to the nearest emergency department immediately. If you have been prescribed an adrenaline injector, use it first and still go.
